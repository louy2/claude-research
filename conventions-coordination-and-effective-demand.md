# Conventions, Coordination, and Effective Demand

## Weaving Schelling and Heath into Keynes' *General Theory*, with Calibrations from the United States, Canada, China, and Japan

*A research synthesis*

---

## 0. Abstract

Keynes' *General Theory of Employment, Interest and Money* (1936) is usually read as a book about aggregate demand. This paper re-reads it as a book about **cooperation under uncertainty**: an economy is a vast, decentralized coordination game, and involuntary unemployment is what a failed equilibrium of that game looks like from the outside. Two later bodies of work supply the micro-machinery Keynes lacked. **Thomas Schelling** shows *how* dispersed agents coordinate without communication — through focal points, conventions, credible commitment, and tipping dynamics — and therefore how they can coordinate on bad outcomes. **Joseph Heath** shows *why* cooperative equilibria hold at all — because human practical reason is norm-governed, not merely instrumental, and because institutions (firms, welfare states, markets themselves) are devices for capturing the benefits of cooperation that uncoordinated exchange leaves on the table. Read through Schelling and Heath, the *General Theory*'s central objects — the state of long-term expectation, the convention of the stock market, liquidity preference, animal spirits, the socialisation of investment — become tractable game-theoretic and normative claims. The paper then assembles the wider literature standing at this intersection, and calibrates the resulting model against four societies whose coordination regimes differ sharply: the United States, Canada, China, and Japan.

## 1. The *General Theory* as a theory of coordination

### 1.1 What Keynes actually claimed

Strip the *General Theory* to its load-bearing propositions and each one is a claim about coordination among strangers:

1. **Effective demand (Ch. 3).** Output and employment are set by what entrepreneurs *expect* to sell, not by what households would like to buy at full employment. Production takes time; entrepreneurs must commit resources today against a guess about everyone else's spending tomorrow. Say's Law fails because there is no mechanism — no auctioneer, no shared plan — that reconciles these guesses in advance.

2. **The two-tier theory of expectation (Chs. 5 and 12).** Short-term expectations govern the utilization of existing capacity and are disciplined by quick feedback. Long-term expectations govern investment and are *not* disciplined by feedback, because "our existing knowledge does not provide a sufficient basis for a calculated mathematical expectation" (GT, Ch. 12). Where calculation gives out, something else must fill the gap.

3. **Convention (Ch. 12).** What fills the gap is a convention: "The essence of this convention … lies in assuming that the existing state of affairs will continue indefinitely, except in so far as we have specific reasons to expect a change." Keynes is explicit that the convention is not a belief anyone holds to be *true*; it is a practice everyone follows because everyone else follows it. Valuations established this way are stable so long as the convention holds — and "precarious" because nothing anchors the convention except itself.

4. **The beauty contest (Ch. 12).** Professional investment is likened to a newspaper competition in which entrants pick not the faces they find prettiest, nor even the faces average opinion finds prettiest, but "what average opinion expects the average opinion to be" — and some, Keynes notes, practice the fourth and fifth degrees. Asset prices are thus the fixed point of an iterated guessing game about other minds, not an estimate of any fundamental.

5. **Animal spirits (Ch. 12).** Because long-term expectation cannot rest on calculation, enterprise depends on "a spontaneous urge to action rather than inaction." Formal decision theory cannot even get the entrepreneur out of bed; if "animal spirits are dimmed and the spontaneous optimism falters, enterprise will fade and die."

6. **Liquidity preference (Chs. 13–17).** Money is the asset you hold when you decline to bet on the conventions governing all other assets. The rate of interest is "a highly conventional … phenomenon" (Ch. 15): it sits where opinion expects it to sit. Hoarding is the individually rational exit from a coordination game whose other players you no longer trust — and if enough players exit at once, the game collapses for everyone. Chapter 17's "essential properties" of money (negligible elasticities of production and substitution) explain why this exit option, unlike a demand for shoes or ships, employs no one.

7. **Unemployment equilibrium (Chs. 18–19).** The economy can come to rest at many levels of activity. Flexible wages do not rescue it, because wage cuts operate on expectations and distribution, not just on a single market's price. There are multiple resting points, and no invisible hand guaranteeing convergence to the good one.

8. **The socialisation of investment (Ch. 24).** If private conventions cannot hold long-term expectation steady, the state must — "a somewhat comprehensive socialisation of investment will prove the only means of securing an approximation to full employment," pursued through "all manner of compromises" between public authority and private initiative.

### 1.2 The missing microfoundation

Keynes asserted all of this in 1936 with no formal apparatus for strategic interaction — von Neumann and Morgenstern were eight years away, Nash sixteen. He could describe conventional expectation with rare literary skill, but he could not say *what kind of object* a convention is, *why* it is stable, *why* it tends to collapse discontinuously rather than eroding smoothly, or *what* policy is doing when it "manages confidence." The standard postwar formalizations (IS-LM, the neoclassical synthesis) kept his accounting identities and set these questions aside.

Game theory after Schelling, and the philosophy of norms after Heath, offer answers. That is the weave attempted here: Schelling supplies the **positive mechanics** of conventions (Section 2), Heath supplies the **normative statics** — why rules bind and what cooperation is for (Section 3) — and Section 4 argues that much of the *General Theory* can be reconstructed by running Schelling's mechanics on Heath's foundations at the scale of a monetary economy.

## 2. Schelling: the mechanics of tacit coordination

*(Core texts: The Strategy of Conflict, 1960; Micromotives and Macrobehavior, 1978.)*

### 2.0 The buried connection: Schelling began as a Keynesian

Before anything else, a historical fact that makes this paper's weave a *reunion* rather than an arranged marriage. Schelling was trained as a Keynesian macroeconomist: his first publications were "Capital Growth and Equilibrium" (*AER*, 1947) and "Income Determination: A Graphic Solution" (*REStat*, 1948), and his first book was *National Income Behavior: An Introduction to Algebraic Analysis* (McGraw-Hill, 1951) — a treatise on Keynesian income determination and multiplier models. Benjamin Wilson's "Keynes Goes Nuclear" (*Modern Intellectual History*, 2021) documents the deeper continuity: Schelling's "The Reciprocal Fear of Surprise Attack" (1958) transplanted the crossed-curve stability analysis and interacting-expectations logic of Keynesian macrodynamics into deterrence theory — bargainers reach agreement "when their expectations jointly converge on an outcome," a self-reinforcing equilibrium framework carried over from macroeconomics. The convergent-expectations equilibrium is a formal thread running through the multiplier, the focal point, the bank run, and mutual deterrence alike. Reading Schelling back into the *General Theory* is therefore not an anachronism; it is completing a circuit that Schelling himself opened from the other end.

### 2.1 Focal points and the possibility of tacit coordination

Schelling's central discovery in *The Strategy of Conflict* is that coordination games with multiple equilibria — which classical game theory declared indeterminate — are routinely solved in practice by **focal points**: solutions salient by precedent, analogy, symmetry, or cultural common knowledge. Two strangers told to meet in New York with no ability to communicate converge on Grand Central at noon. The equilibrium selected is not the "best" one; it is the *conspicuous* one.

This is very close to the object Keynes needed. The Chapter 12 convention — project the present forward — has the structure of a focal point in the space of possible forecasting rules: it requires no information anyone lacks, it is symmetric, and each investor's adherence is justified by everyone else's. The beauty contest is a focal-point search made explicit: at the fixed point of "what average opinion expects average opinion to be" sits whatever valuation is *salient*, which is normally yesterday's price. Keynes' remark that a conventional valuation is established by "the mass psychology of a large number of ignorant individuals" reads, after Schelling, as a mechanism rather than a sneer: when nobody can calculate, salience becomes the default equilibrium-selection device. (A caution flagged in Section 6: Davis and the post-Keynesian literature argue that Lewis-style equilibrium accounts of convention presuppose a stability of common knowledge that Keynesian uncertainty denies — the fit is productive, not seamless.)

Three Schelling corollaries matter for macroeconomics:

- **Conventions are self-enforcing but not self-justifying.** Each participant's conformity is a best response to others' conformity; nothing about the convention need track fundamentals. Bad conventions (overvaluation, panic pricing, a deflationary anchor) can be as stable as good ones — nothing in the selection mechanism favors the good ones.
- **Convention change tends to be discontinuous.** Because the convention's main support is mutual expectation, contrary evidence often fails to erode it gradually; it holds, and then it *breaks*, when some event makes it common knowledge that common knowledge has been lost. This gives Keynes' "violent" revisions of valuation a mechanism.
- **Focal points can be supplied.** A third party loud enough to be heard by everyone can *create* salience — which is what a central bank's announced target, a peg, a deposit guarantee, or "whatever it takes" actually does. Confidence policy is focal-point engineering (developed in Section 4.4).

### 2.2 Commitment: the strategy of constraining yourself

Schelling's second great theme is that in interdependent decision, **the power to bind oneself is a strategic asset**: the side that visibly forecloses its own options moves the equilibrium. Burn the bridge behind you and your threat to stand becomes credible.

Much of macroeconomic policy is applied commitment theory. A central bank's problem is that its future self will be tempted to accommodate; institutional independence, mandates, and rules are Schelling-style bridge-burning that make the announced focal point credible. Conversely, Keynes' Chapter 24 case for the socialisation of investment is a commitment argument: private investors *cannot* commit to sustained investment (each is free to flee to liquidity at any moment, and knows the others are too), whereas a state, "calculating the marginal efficiency of capital-goods on long views and on the basis of the general social advantage," can. The state's comparative advantage is not information; it is the ability to be *un-nimble* — to be the player who cannot run, and around whom the others can therefore coordinate.

### 2.3 Micromotives, tipping, and the aggregation problem

*Micromotives and Macrobehavior* is Schelling's study of how individually innocuous choices aggregate into collectively unchosen outcomes — the checkerboard segregation model, critical-mass dynamics, the dying seminar whose attendance depends on attendance ("nearly everybody, if asked, alleges that he'd have continued attending pretty regularly if enough others had cared enough to attend regularly"). Its master concept is the **tipping point**: systems whose participants condition on each other's behavior have thresholds, and cross-threshold dynamics are self-accelerating. Two details of the book confirm how macroeconomic its imagination remained. Chapter 2, "The Inescapable Mathematics of Musical Chairs," is a meditation on closed-system accounting identities — every purchase is a sale; one child is always chairless however alert the children — the arithmetic constraints within which fallacy-of-composition results like the paradox of thrift arise. (The identity alone does not deliver the paradox; it takes the behavioral coupling of Chapter 3's models to do that. Schelling keeps the two rigorously separate, which is itself a lesson macroeconomic rhetoric often needs.) And Chapter 3's taxonomy of **self-fulfilling prophecies** takes its canonical cases straight from Keynes' decade: "when people believed that a bank was on the verge of insolvency they hurried to withdraw their deposits, provoking the insolvency they feared," and the expected shortage that causes the hoarding that causes the shortage. Schelling even adds two variants macro theory still underuses: the *self-displacing* prophecy (everyone aims slightly above the average, so the average escalates without limit — a fine model of a bubble's drift phase) and the *self-negating* prophecy (everyone expects the crowd and stays home).

This is the formal home of the *General Theory*'s most distinctive claims:

- **The paradox of thrift is a Schelling diagram.** Each household's saving decision conditions on expected income; each firm's hiring conditions on expected demand. Individually prudent retrenchment, aggregated, produces the collective outcome nobody chose — lower income and often lower total saving. Keynes' fallacy-of-composition arguments are micromotives/macrobehavior arguments.
- **The marginal propensity to consume is a coupling coefficient.** The multiplier measures how tightly one agent's spending is wired to another's income — that is, how fast disturbances propagate through the interaction structure. High coupling means both strong policy leverage and strong crash dynamics.
- **Animal spirits have critical mass.** Investment booms and collapses behave like Schelling's attendance model: above a threshold of participating optimists, optimism is self-confirming and recruits; below it, pessimism is. The "state of confidence" is then not only a mood spreading person-to-person (Shiller's epidemiology captures that channel); it is also, and more structurally, the region of a threshold model the economy currently occupies — which is why confidence can change faster than any contagion process would allow.

### 2.4 What Schelling cannot supply

Schelling's framework is deliberately austere about motivation: agents best-respond to expectations. But a pure best-response account of convention has a well-known instability — if conformity is only ever instrumentally rational, then every convention is hostage to every fluctuation in expectation, and the miracle is not that conventions break but that they ever hold. Keynes saw this ("a practical theory of the future … subject to sudden and violent changes"), and Schelling can describe the breaking beautifully. What neither explains is the observed *stickiness*: why do workers not accept money-wage cuts, why do firms maintain price and employment relationships through slumps, why does anyone honor obligations when panic would pay? For that we need an account of agents who follow rules *as rules*. That is Heath.

## 3. Heath: why cooperation holds, and what it is for

*(Core texts: Communicative Action and Rational Choice, 2001; The Efficient Society, 2001; "The Benefits of Cooperation," 2006; Following the Rules, 2008; Filthy Lucre, 2009 / Economics Without Illusions, 2010; "Three Normative Models of the Welfare State," 2011; Morality, Competition, and the Firm, 2014; The Machinery of Government, 2020; Cooperation and Social Justice, 2022.)*

### 3.1 Norm-following as the solution to the instability problem

Heath's *Following the Rules* argues that purely instrumental rationality is self-undermining in social settings: it cannot generate the assurance that cooperation requires (the folk-theorem multiplicity means "rational" agents don't know *which* equilibrium they are in), and it licenses defection wherever monitoring fails. Actual human practical reason, Heath argues, is **deontically constrained**: people treat social norms as directly reason-giving — they follow rules *because they are the rules*, not because each act of conformity passes a cost-benefit test. Norm-conformity is a disposition with its own motivational force, culturally transmitted and sanction-stabilized, and it is this non-instrumental residue that makes large-scale cooperation among strangers possible.

Grafted onto Keynes, this yields the missing account of conventional *stability*:

- Keynes' Chapter 12 convention holds not merely because each investor calculates that others will hold it (Schelling's mechanism, which is real but fragile) but because market participants inhabit a **normatively thick practice** — accounting standards, fiduciary duties, professional valuation customs, the entire etiquette of "sound finance" — that makes projecting the present forward the *proper* thing to do, not just the expedient thing.
- Keynes' famous observation that workers resist money-wage cuts while accepting real-wage erosion through prices (Ch. 2) — long treated as an embarrassment ("money illusion") — becomes, through Heath, intelligible as rational at the level of the practice: relative money wages are the *fairness convention* of the labor market, a norm that stabilizes cooperation between firms and workers. Firms decline to cut wages in slumps for the same reason. Wage stickiness is not friction; it is the normative infrastructure of ongoing cooperation doing its job. (Truman Bewley's interview evidence and Akerlof's fair-wage models fit naturally here.)
- Conversely, Heath's framework predicts *where* conventions will be fragile: in arenas deliberately stripped of normative thickness — anonymous, exit-friendly, purely instrumental arenas. Keynes named the arena: the more the stock exchange approximates a frictionless casino of strangers, "the risk of the predominance of speculation does … increase" (Ch. 12, with the famous comparison of Wall Street to a casino and his musing about making purchases "permanent and indissoluble, like marriage"). Keynes' proposed transfer taxes are, in Heath's terms, an attempt to re-thicken a practice that liquidity had thinned.

### 3.2 The benefits of cooperation: a taxonomy for macroeconomics

In "The Benefits of Cooperation" (*Philosophy & Public Affairs*, 2006) Heath catalogs the five distinct mechanisms by which cooperation makes everyone better off — **economies of scale, gains from trade, risk pooling, self-binding, and information transmission** — and insists that different institutions are specialized vehicles for different mechanisms (corporations for scale, markets and property for trade, insurance and the welfare state for risk pooling, constitutions and families for self-binding, science and media for information), each with its characteristic free-rider pathology (shirking, moral hazard, adverse selection, lying). His pointed diagnosis is that contract theory and welfare economics suffer a **"catallactic bias"**: a tacit privileging of gains-from-trade — the invisible-hand theorem — as *the* mechanism of cooperative benefit, with the idealized conditions of the welfare theorems in effect assuming the other four mechanisms away. In "Three Normative Models of the Welfare State" (2011) he completes the argument: of the redistributive, communitarian, and public-economic reconstructions of the welfare state, the *efficiency* one — the state as the institution whose unique asset, compulsory universal membership, lets it resolve collective-action problems and pool risks that markets cannot (moral hazard, adverse selection) — best explains what welfare states actually do. The welfare state is not charity or vote-buying; it is "a huge insurance company with an army" (Krugman's line, which Heath adopts as epigraph), i.e., the capture of efficiency gains that private insurance cannot reach.

Keynes can be read as a major casualty of the catallactic bias Heath diagnoses: the postwar synthesis rendered the *General Theory* as a story about one mechanism (gains from trade, obstructed by sticky prices), when much of the book concerns the other mechanisms failing — risk pooling (uninsurable uncertainty), self-binding (no private commitment to sustained investment), and information transmission (the beauty contest degrading price signals into mirrors). (The fourth mapping — demand complementarity as an analogue of economies of scale — is looser, and is flagged as such.)

This taxonomy reorganizes Keynes:

- **Uninsurable uncertainty is a missing risk-pooling market.** Keynes' fundamental uncertainty ("we simply do not know") means the private economy cannot pool the risk that matters most — the risk that the *aggregate* future disappoints. Precautionary saving and liquidity preference are each agent's makeshift self-insurance against a risk that is, in the aggregate, only worsened by everyone self-insuring at once (the paradox of thrift, again). The Keynesian state — automatic stabilizers, deposit insurance, unemployment insurance, lender of last resort — is Heath's risk-pooling logic applied to *macroeconomic* risk: the one insurer whose pool is the whole game.
- **Effective demand is a network of gains from trade that can fail to be realized.** Involuntary unemployment is Heath's "suboptimal equilibrium" at economy scale: mutually beneficial trades (idle workers, unmet wants) that no bilateral action can consummate, because each trade is profitable only if the others occur. This is the market-failure vocabulary applied to the macro whole — and it is why Heath's "market failures approach" to ethics has a natural macro extension: the duty of price/wage forbearance in a panic, or of governments to stabilize, is the duty not to exploit a coordination failure.
- **Self-binding is Chapter 24.** Heath's fourth benefit of cooperation — institutions as commitment devices — is Schelling's commitment theory in normative dress, and it is what "a somewhat comprehensive socialisation of investment" provides: a standing, rule-bound commitment that aggregate investment will not be permitted to collapse, which stabilizes private long-term expectation by being *non-optional*.

### 3.3 Efficiency, not warmth: the Heathian reading of Keynesian policy

Heath's signature polemical move — in *The Efficient Society* and *Filthy Lucre* — is to defend cooperative institutions on **efficiency** grounds, refusing both the right's equation of markets with efficiency and the left's suspicion of it. Canada's single-payer health care is defended not as compassion but as the cheaper, Pareto-superior arrangement; likewise deposit insurance, public pensions, and financial regulation.

This is also a natural reading of Keynes, who was no socialist: the *General Theory* ends by insisting that its purpose is to *save* the market order's "efficiency and freedom" from its one great remediable defect, the failure to coordinate investment at full employment. Keynes and Heath share the same architecture: markets are magnificent cooperative technology for allocating; they systematically fail at *assuring* — at holding expectations steady enough for the allocating to proceed — and the state's macro role is to supply the assurance, not to replace the allocation. Both are, in this sense, theorists of the **mixed economy as the efficient solution to the assurance problem**, against both laissez-faire and planning.

## 4. The weave: a Schelling–Heath–Keynes model of the macroeconomy

### 4.1 The economy as a monetary stag hunt

Assemble the pieces. Model the economy as a many-player **assurance game (stag hunt)** played repeatedly under fundamental uncertainty:

- Each agent (firm, household, investor) chooses between **Commit** — invest, hire, spend, hold illiquid claims on the future — and **Withdraw** — hoard liquidity, delay, self-insure.
- Payoffs are interdependent through demand: Commit pays well iff enough others Commit (the stag); Withdraw pays a modest certain return regardless (the hare).
- **Money is what makes the hare available.** In a barter economy, "withdrawing" would itself be a demand for something producible. Keynes' Chapter 17 point — money is the asset with negligible elasticity of production, which private effort cannot create more of when demanded — is, on this reading, what turns the macroeconomy from a game with one equilibrium into a stag hunt with (at least) two: a high-commitment, high-employment equilibrium and a low-commitment, unemployment equilibrium. *In this model, involuntary unemployment is the hare equilibrium of a monetary economy.*
- **Fundamental uncertainty strips away the standard selection arguments.** Even with calculable risk, stag hunts resist confident selection — the risk-dominance literature shows that "rational" players may well pick the hare. Keynesian uncertainty removes even the materials those refinements work with: equilibrium selection must run on Schelling salience and Heath norms instead, and the economy sits wherever **convention** currently points. (This makes the model's Keynesian pessimism *stronger*, not weaker, than the risk-based version: there is no presumption that decentralized reasoning finds the stag.)

The three authors then divide the labor as follows:

| Layer | Question | Keynes' term | Supplied by |
|---|---|---|---|
| Payoff structure | Why are there multiple equilibria at all? | Monetary economy; liquidity (Ch. 17) | Keynes |
| Equilibrium selection | Which equilibrium do we land on? | Convention, beauty contest, state of confidence (Ch. 12) | Schelling: focal points, salience |
| Dynamics | How do we move between them? | Violent revision, animal spirits, multiplier | Schelling: tipping, critical mass, coupling |
| Stability | Why doesn't selection dissolve into constant flux? | "The essence of this convention"; sticky wages (Ch. 2) | Heath: deontic constraint, normative thickness |
| Purpose & policy | What is the cooperative surplus and who underwrites it? | Full employment; socialisation of investment (Ch. 24) | Heath: benefits of cooperation, risk pooling; Schelling: commitment |

### 4.2 Confidence, formally

"The state of confidence" — Keynes admits businessmen watch it obsessively while economists cannot define it — becomes definable: **confidence is the subjective probability each agent assigns to sufficient aggregate Commitment, given the current convention and its normative backing.** It has three distinct supports, which the model treats as separate parameters:

1. **σ (salience/authority):** the clarity and authority of the reigning focal point — is there one obvious thing to expect? (Schelling)
2. **ν (normative thickness):** the degree to which commitment is norm-governed rather than continuously recalculated — how much contrary news does it take before agents *permit themselves* to defect? (Heath)
3. **λ (liquidity pull):** the attractiveness and availability of the exit option — the certain payoff of Withdraw. (Keynes)

A slump is any event that lowers σ (the focal point is discredited), erodes ν (defection becomes normal), or raises λ (safety pays better) past the tipping threshold. The three parameters also define the **policy space**, and — as Section 5 argues — they are the parameters along which the US, Canada, China, and Japan most visibly differ. (A refinement discovered later in the paper, flagged here so the triad is not over-trusted: the formalizations of Appendix B find that σ and ν are each composites of two distinct formal objects — informational precision versus coordinating prominence for σ, incumbent-attached versus content-laden norms for ν. Section 5 puts the split to work: which *component* of σ and ν a society holds turns out to differentiate the four countries as much as how much of each; B.3 records where the components come apart formally.)

### 4.3 Crisis as convention collapse

The model's crisis sequence, composited from 1929, 1990 (Japan), 2008 (US), and 2021 (China property):

1. A thin-ν arena (speculative asset market: the most anonymous, exit-friendly, purely instrumental arena in the economy) runs the beauty contest to an extreme valuation.
2. An event makes the loss of common knowledge itself common knowledge — Schelling's discontinuity. The convention does not adjust; it *vacates*. ("The practice of calmness and immobility, of certainty and security, suddenly breaks down," as Keynes put it in the 1937 QJE restatement.)
3. With σ destroyed, agents fall back on the only focal point that needs no coordination: **liquidity** (λ dominates). Flight to money is the one move you can make without guessing what others think.
4. The tipping dynamics of Section 2.3 propagate the withdrawal through demand coupling (multiplier), and the economy settles at the hare equilibrium.
5. Recovery has no reliable spontaneous mechanism, because no private agent can unilaterally re-supply σ: re-committing alone is simply losing the stag hunt by yourself. Someone must (a) be *loud* enough to create a new focal point, (b) be *bound* enough for the focal point to be credible, and (c) *pool the risk* of the transition. (a) is Schelling salience, (b) is Schelling commitment, (c) is Heath risk-pooling — and in most societies the only agent plausibly possessing all three at economy scale is the state. This turns Chapter 24 from an assertion into an argument (not a derivation: the premises — that no private coalition can assemble the three — are empirical, and Section 5's China case shows the state can also *fail* the credibility condition).

### 4.4 Policy as coordination engineering

The weave reclassifies the Keynesian toolkit by mechanism rather than instrument:

- **Focal-point supply (σ):** inflation targets, forward guidance, exchange-rate pegs, "whatever it takes," five-year plans, announced public investment pipelines. These work *when believed* by giving the beauty contest a new answer — policy as the loud third party of Section 2.1.
- **Commitment manufacture (σ-credibility):** central-bank independence, fiscal rules with escape clauses, treaty obligations, automatic stabilizers (commitment mechanized — no discretion to fail to respond).
- **Norm maintenance (ν):** financial regulation as practice-thickening (Keynes' transfer tax; margin rules; the post-2008 macroprudential turn); wage-bargaining architecture; the ethics of forbearance in crisis (Heath's market-failures approach applied to panics: don't exploit the fire sale).
- **Exit-option management (λ):** the interest rate as the price of the hare; deposit insurance and lender-of-last-resort as *detoxifying* the exit (making safety available without demand collapse); in the limit, negative rates and cash restrictions as raising the hare's cost.
- **Direct commitment of demand (bypassing selection):** public investment — the state simply *plays Commit* at scale, raising the payoff to everyone else's Commit until the private tipping threshold is crossed. This is why fiscal policy can work when announcements fail: it asks much less of belief — though not nothing, since its effect still depends on the private sector expecting the commitment to persist rather than to be reversed or taxed back.

### 4.5 What the weave predicts (and the postwar synthesis didn't)

1. **Discontinuity:** confidence crises are typically cliff events rather than smooth deteriorations (convention collapse, not parameter drift).
2. **Hysteresis of conventions:** after a collapse, the *new* convention (e.g., "deflation is normal," "property only rises") is itself sticky — slumps and bubbles both self-perpetuate. Recovery of output does not automatically recover ν or σ.
3. **Institution-dependence:** identical shocks produce different macro dynamics in societies with different σ, ν, λ — there is no institution-free macroeconomics. (This is the license for Section 5.)
4. **Limits of monetarism:** where ν and σ are damaged, interest-rate policy mostly re-prices an exit everyone has already taken (Japan, 1995–2012; the liquidity trap as coordination failure, not a technical floor).
5. **The casino conjecture:** the more purely instrumental (thin-ν, high-liquidity) an asset market's culture, the more violent its conventional revisions — testable across financial systems (Section 5).

A caveat on the word "predicts": the crisis cases used throughout (1929, Japan 1990, US 2008, China 2021) also informed the model's construction. Section 5 is therefore calibration and illustration — a demonstration that the framework organizes the cases coherently — not out-of-sample confirmation. The genuinely testable content is the comparative structure (same shock, different regimes, different dynamics) and the research agenda in Section 7.

## 5. Calibration: four institutional assignments, four coordination regimes

*(This section states the calibrated model; the empirical anchors and full sources are gathered in Appendix A. Data appear here only as brief anchors.)*

The task of this section is to connect Heath's institutional analysis to the (σ, ν, λ) model of Section 4 — focal points, norms, exit; hereafter the **FNE model**. The claim is not that four macro histories fit three parameters; it is that each country's FNE profile can be *derived* from its institutional assignment of Heath's five mechanisms of cooperative benefit (Section 3.2): economies of scale, gains from trade, risk pooling, self-binding, and information transmission. The four societies make a useful comparative set because their assignments differ along all five rows — with the honest proviso that four cases selected with the model in mind constitute an illustration and a plausibility probe, not an experiment.

**The method is Heath's, not the comparativist's.** Following *The Efficient Society*, societies differ less in their values than in which institution — market, firm, network, family, or state — they entrust with each mechanism. Three derivation rules carry the section. λ is high wherever household risk pooling lacks a carrier: self-insurance through liquidity is what agents do when no institution pools their risks. ν is thick wherever mechanisms are embedded in ongoing relational carriers rather than spot contracts. σ resides wherever the society has located information transmission about the future — prices, consensus institutions, or the state. Throughout, the section uses the refinements of Appendix B.3: σ has an *informational-precision* component (public signals, which can amplify volatility) and a *pure-prominence* component (a correlation device, subject to a credibility constraint); ν comes in *incumbent-attached* form (defending whatever convention reigns, good or bad) and *content-laden* form (favoring a particular action). Which component a country has turns out to matter as much as how much. One preliminary point from the welfare-regimes literature sharpens the whole exercise: Esping-Andersen (1990) classifies the United States and Canada in the same "liberal" world, yet their macro-coordination characters diverge — regime typology underdetermines assignment, which is why the audit must go row by row.

**A note on measurement.** The survey instruments disagree, informatively. WVS generalized trust runs China 63.5 > Canada 46.7 > US 37.0 > Japan 33.7 — contradicting both Fukuyama (US and Japan high-trust, China familistic) and Edelman (2025: China 77; Japan *lowest* in the sample at 37). These instruments measure different objects — stated generalized trust (in China's case plausibly narrow-radius and survey-inflated), sentiment toward named institutions — and none measures ν. ν is the **thickness of the practices that actually govern commitment**, observable in behavior: does the wage round bind, does the firm do layoffs, does the bank get rescued? Japan is the clearest exhibit: last or near-last on stated trust, yet the tightest culture Gelfand's instrument measured among large advanced economies (8.6, vs. China 7.9 and the US 5.1) and organizational commitment practices among the thickest anywhere. High ν does not mean people say they trust; it means defection from the practice is not among the live options. The calibration accordingly reads institutions and behavior, using surveys only as texture.

### 5.1 United States: prices as focal-point supplier

The American assignment is the most lopsided of the four: one mechanism, gains from trade, is given the widest scope of any case, and markets are asked to carry functions other societies assign elsewhere.

**The assignment.** Information transmission about the future is assigned to *prices*: an industry of analysts, ratings, and disclosure exists to make the stock market the economy's forecasting organ (market capitalization 216% of GDP in 2024 — the arena on which Keynes calibrated Chapter 12). Risk pooling is deliberately residual — employer-tied health insurance, means-tested programs, employment-at-will — the "liberal" pattern in Esping-Andersen's typology, which the Estévez-Abe–Iversen–Soskice chapter in *Varieties of Capitalism* (2001) links to production: where social protection does not insure skill investments, workers rationally hold general, portable skills, and commitment to any particular match stays shallow. Self-binding is concentrated in nearly one institution, the Federal Reserve (trust in the elected government having fallen from 73% in 1958 to 17–22% in 2024–25). And the banking structure itself is politically chosen: Calomiris and Haber (2014) reconstruct it as the outcome of a "Game of Bank Bargains" — a populist coalition that long favored fragmented unit banking, buying local credit at the price of chronic fragility.

**The FNE derivation.** The American σ is dominated by its *informational-precision* component: an enormous flow of public signals about fundamentals. B.3's two-edged result applies directly — precise public information tightens coordination and amplifies overreaction to public noise, so American σ is powerful and volatile at once, with the Fed supplying *pure prominence* as patcher when the price-based component fails. ν is thin and largely *content-laden* in the pro-market direction: the norms that exist license recontracting rather than defend incumbency, which is why American conventions turn over quickly in both directions. λ is high — and institutionally produced, not temperamental: Garon (2012) documents that the US, nearly alone among rich countries, built institutions to promote spending and credit rather than saving (household saving 4.6% in 2024), atop the unpooled-risk residual that makes liquidity each household's makeshift insurer.

**The uncarried margin.** Household risk pooling has no competent carrier, and the price-based information channel is corruptible. The characteristic crisis follows: 2008 was the forecasting organ converted into a machine for exploiting information asymmetries, in a banking structure bargained into fragility (1907, the 1930s, 2008), with unpooled households amplifying the withdrawal.

**The policy margin.** σ-repair (Fed credibility) plus ν-thickening (macroprudential rules as practice-thickening). The FNE model adds a warning the standard debate misses: λ will not fall by exhortation while risk pooling remains residual — the saving rate is an institutional output.

### 5.2 Canada: same regime type, different assignment

Canada is the demonstration case for the method — Esping-Andersen's typology puts it in the American box, and its macro character nonetheless differs, because two rows of the assignment differ. In this comparison Canada functions almost as a control case — "almost," because countries are not randomized.

**The assignment.** Risk pooling is assigned to the state, on Heath's own efficiency grounds: single-payer health insurance (Canada Health Act) and constitutionally entrenched equalization (s. 36(2)) pool household and regional risk that fragmented private insurance cannot reach. Finance reflects a different bank bargain: where the American game produced fragmentation, Calomiris and Haber count zero systemic crises in Canada since 1840 against a dozen in the US, tracing the difference to a chartered branch-banking oligopoly under federal oversight — the 1867 design Bordo, Redish and Rockoff analyze. Stability is the joint franchise interest of a small club with too much charter value to gamble.

**The FNE derivation.** σ is mixed and stable: moderate informational precision, with credible *pure prominence* held by federal institutions — B.3's credibility constraint is comfortably met, since the follower population (a handful of banks, one regulator) is small and repeat-playing. ν is high, institutional, and mostly *incumbent-attached*: the norms defend the reigning arrangement as such. λ is managed — households whose catastrophic risks are pooled do not need to self-insure through hoarding.

**The uncarried margin.** The grid has no unassigned row, which is why Canada's recessions arrive by import (2008 came through trade, not domestic convention collapse: no bank failures, no equity bailouts — liquidity support, yes — and the WEF's "soundest banking" ranking 2007–2013). But two drawdowns deserve flagging. Household debt-to-income now sits among the OECD's highest — an unmanaged λ-vulnerability accumulating on the mortgage margin, where the oligopoly's stability and household leverage feed each other. And Banting and Myles (2013) document the fading of redistributive politics: the risk-pooling assignment is being maintained but not renewed.

**The policy margin.** Maintenance — with the B.3 caveat that incumbent-attached ν produces symmetric hysteresis: the same thickness that protects a good convention would entrench a bad one, and erosion of the pooling assignment would be hard to observe until tested.

### 5.3 Japan: the relational welfare state and its missing reinsurer

Japan assigned the mechanisms neither to markets nor to the state but to the **firm and the inter-firm network**. Calling this a *relational welfare state* is this paper's framing, not a term found in the sources — but the comparative literature comes close: Estévez-Abe (2008) shows postwar Japan achieved egalitarian outcomes with meager social spending by routing protection through "functional equivalents" embedded in employment, and Dore (2000) describes the same configuration as employee-favoring welfare capitalism against the Anglo-American shareholder variety.

**The assignment.** Read each classic institution as the mechanism it carries. Lifetime employment is firm-provided unemployment insurance — and, per Estévez-Abe–Iversen–Soskice, the insurance that made deep firm-specific skill investment rational: risk pooling and commitment are the same institution seen from two sides. The main bank is a privately supplied lender of last resort; keiretsu cross-shareholding is mutual self-binding by exchange of hostages; shuntō is coordinated information transmission — an annual synchronized announcement of the wage-price convention. Aoki formalized why the bundle held: the complementarities run in a circle, making this arguably the thickest-ν capitalism yet constructed.

**The FNE derivation.** Japanese σ is nearly all *pure prominence*, located in consensus institutions, with low informational precision — information transmission ran through relationships (main-bank monitoring) rather than prices, so asset prices could stay wrong longer, with less error-correction, than a price-based system would tolerate. The bubble and the thick ν were the same institution. ν is very high and — the decisive B.3 point — *incumbent-attached*: the norms defend whatever convention currently reigns. The Japan thesis is therefore a conditional theorem, and the condition checks: after 1990 (Nikkei peak 38,916 on 29 December 1989, not regained until February 2024), "minimize debt, defend employment" itself became the prescribed practice — Koo's balance-sheet recession re-derived as firms paying their insurance claims. λ is entrenched *stock*-λ: household wealth parked in currency and deposits, a portfolio Garon shows was itself institutionally cultivated (postal savings, national thrift campaigns) rather than culturally given; flow saving had fallen to roughly 1.5% by 2023.

**The uncarried margin.** The relational welfare state has no reinsurer. Firm-carried insurance pools idiosyncratic risk well and aggregate risk not at all; when the aggregate shock came, every firm was the insurer of its workers and clients with no one insuring *it*, and honoring the micro-obligations required macro-withdrawal. Hence the model's account of why zero rates from February 1999 onward did so little: λ-pricing cannot help when Withdraw is what honoring the convention requires.

**The policy margin.** Escape required σ-rupture — the state seizing prominence from exhausted consensus institutions (Abenomics' regime-change rhetoric, completed by the 2022–24 global inflation shock), with shuntō broadcasting the *new* convention (5.24% in 2024, the first round above 5% since 1991; roughly 5.4% in 2025). B.3's sequencing theorem is Japan quantified: thick incumbent-attached ν raises the σ needed for escape — norm-bound societies are harder to talk out of a slump. Meanwhile the bundle has been unwinding not by rupture but by the gradual modes Streeck and Thelen (2005) catalogue — drift and layering: non-regular employment at 36.8% of workers (2024) layered beside the lifetime core, cross-held shares below 10% of holdings by 2017, classic main-bank monitoring defunct after the 2000s consolidations. The Japan that escaped the deflation convention has structurally thinner ν than the one that got locked into it.

### 5.4 China: monopoly σ and the unbindable guarantor

China's assignment is the inverse of America's: nearly everything to the **state** at macro scale, to the **family and particularist network** at micro scale, with little in between. The characterization has independent support: Pearson, Rithmire and Tsai (2021) argue "state capitalism" now understates the case — under *party-state capitalism*, party survival ranks above developmental goals, and party power disciplines private capital directly.

**The assignment.** Economies of scale run through SOEs and plans; gains from trade through administered markets steered by industrial policy and window guidance (Naughton 2018 gives the standard account; the PBOC's structural facilities alone exceeded RMB 7 trillion by end-2024); information transmission through the state — targets, plans, and the implicit guarantee ("the government will not let X fall") as the master convention. Below, contract enforcement runs on guanxi — Greif's collectivist equilibrium, consistent with Fukuyama's familism and with the narrow-radius reading of China's WVS score. Two rows are empty, and they generate the profile.

**The FNE derivation.** Chinese σ is nearly pure *prominence* at monopoly scale — a correlation device of great power (the 2008–10 stimulus was arguably the largest deliberate re-coordination of a national stag hunt on record) with little independent informational precision, since public signals are themselves policy outputs. But B.3's credibility constraint binds hardest on a monopolist: an announcement works only if enough agents expect enough others to follow. ν is particularist — guanxi and family below, guarantee above, thin civic middle. λ is extreme: household risk pooling has no carrier (the guarantee reaches producers — developers, SOEs, local-government vehicles — not households, whose education, health, housing, and old-age risks remain substantially privatized), and households respond as Heath's taxonomy predicts, with self-insurance at civilization scale — gross national saving of 42.5–43% of GDP (2023), urban household saving around 30–35% of disposable income (Chamon and Prasad, and the IMF literature after them). Garon's point applies here too: decades of state thrift promotion built the saving infrastructure — but the insurance gap is what keeps it full.

**The uncarried margin.** Self-binding has no carrier at all — and here the paper's "unbindable guarantor" is again its own framing, though North and Weingast (1989) supply the nearest analogue: a sovereign becomes credible only when constrained by institutions it cannot cheaply override, which is what England bought in 1689 and what a party-state cannot institutionalize without ceasing to be one (independent courts, entrenched entitlements, a central bank that can say no). The master convention therefore rested on a guarantee known to be revocable at discretion; the Three Red Lines (August 2020) were the revocation. What followed — Evergrande's default in December 2021 with over $300 billion in liabilities and liquidation order in January 2024, thirty-plus developer defaults, the 2022 mortgage boycotts on unfinished pre-sold homes (conditional cooperation running in reverse, in strikingly clean form), household retrenchment and deflationary drift persisting even as the Red Lines were quietly retired — reads as convention collapse without the market-panic phase but with the full withdrawal aftermath. Housing, roughly 70% of household wealth, had functioned for two decades as a state-salience beauty contest; it could not be revived by announcement. Appendix B derives the reason as a theorem schema: **the σ-monopolist cannot re-inflate by announcement a convention it revoked by discretion** — credibility cannot be self-supplied.

**The policy margin.** λ-reduction via risk pooling: health, pensions, hukou reform (the July 2024 State Council urbanization action plan is a start) lower λ structurally without asking anything of belief — B.3's finding that λ is the cleanest, least belief-dependent lever, applied to the one case where the belief-dependent levers are the broken ones. The harder margin is constructing self-binding devices the sovereign visibly cannot cheaply override; on the North–Weingast reading, that is a constitutional problem wearing a macroeconomic costume.

### 5.5 The comparative tables

The Heathian audit compresses into one question asked five times per country: *to which institution is each mechanism of cooperative benefit assigned?*

| Heath mechanism | United States | Canada | Japan | China |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Economies of scale | Corporations (equity-financed) | Corporations | Firm groups (keiretsu) | SOEs, plans |
| Gains from trade | Markets (maximal scope) | Markets | Markets, relationally embedded | Administered markets + guanxi |
| Risk pooling | **Residual** (employer-tied, means-tested) | State (single payer, equalization) | Firm (lifetime employment, main bank) | State for producers; **unassigned for households** |
| Self-binding | Federal Reserve (nearly alone) | Federal institutions, bank franchise | Cross-shareholding (mutual hostages) | **Unassigned** (no device binds the sovereign) |
| Information transmission | Prices (market as forecasting organ) | Mixed | Relationships (main bank, shuntō) | State (plans, targets, guarantee) |

The bolded cells — each society's unassigned or residual mechanisms — are where its characteristic crisis lives: information transmission corrupted and risk unpooled (US, 2008); firm-carried insurance with no reinsurer (Japan, 1990s); the unpooled household and the unbindable guarantor (China, 2021–). Canada's row of no bolded cells is the Heathian restatement of its near-control status — and its accumulating household-debt vulnerability sits, consistently, in the one place its configuration leaves thinnest. The FNE profile of each society is this table's projection onto three coordinates:

| Parameter | United States | Canada | Japan | China |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Focal-point supply (σ) | Market-generated, volatile; Fed as patcher | Mixed, stable | Consensus institutions; slow to change | State monopoly; powerful, single point of failure |
| Normative thickness (ν) | Thin, contractual | High, institutional | Very high, relational-organizational | Particularist (guanxi) + state guarantee |
| Exit pull (λ) | High-glamour financial liquidity | Managed (risk pooled) | Entrenched stock-λ (deposit-heavy wealth; corporate debt-minimization) | Extreme precautionary saving (insurance gap) |
| Characteristic failure | Speculative convention collapse (1929, 2008) | Imported recessions | Convention lock-in at bad equilibrium (1990s–2010s) | Focal-point rupture by the supplier (2021–) |
| Characteristic strength | Fast re-coordination, deep re-allocation | Crisis avoidance | Long-horizon commitment | Mobilization at will |
| Keynes chapter it illustrates | Ch. 12 (beauty contest) | Ch. 24 (the compromise working) | Ch. 17 + liquidity trap (Ch. 15) | Ch. 12 + Ch. 24 fused in one actor |
| Effective policy margin | σ-repair (Fed credibility) + ν-thickening (macroprudential) | Maintenance | σ-rupture of the bad convention (Abenomics) | λ-reduction via risk pooling (welfare state) |

Read with the B.3 refinements, the σ row splits informatively — the US holds informational precision, China pure prominence, Japan prominence lodged in consensus institutions, Canada a stable mix — and the ν row splits likewise, with Japan and Canada carrying incumbent-attached thickness (hence hysteresis, benign in one case and malignant in the other) while American norms are thin but content-laden toward recontracting. No society has yet assigned all five mechanisms to competent carriers, and each society's characteristic macro-pathology is the pathology of its weakest assignment. Two honesty notes belong here. First, the organizing labels "relational welfare state" and "unbindable guarantor" are this paper's synthesis, organizing rather than reporting the cited literature — though Estévez-Abe's functional-equivalents thesis and North and Weingast's sovereign-commitment problem come close enough to suggest the framings are not eccentric. Second, the conclusion the table forces is the one Iversen and Soskice (2019) reach by another route when they argue that advanced capitalism depends on continuous, state-supplied preconditions: assurance is never self-maintaining. There is, therefore, no universal Keynesianism. The *General Theory*'s policy chapters are a menu, and the correct item is determined by the audit — which mechanism is locally uncarried, and which institution could carry it. The comparative question is never "which country has the right values?" but Heath's: which collective-action problems has each country solved, at what price, and which has it left on the table?

## 6. The wider intersection: a guide to the literature

*(Annotated; full citations in Section 9. Grouped by the role each body of work plays in the weave. A note on novelty: a systematic search finds **almost no existing scholarship whose central project is connecting Schelling to Keynes, and none connecting Heath to Keynes or to macroeconomics at all**. The exceptions prove instructive: Wilson (2021) establishes the *historical* Schelling–Keynes link (Schelling's game theory grew out of his Keynesian training — see Section 2.0) but does not build the theoretical weave forward into macroeconomics; Lanteri & Carabelli (2011) argue that Keynes' original beauty contest is closer to a Schelling-style focal-point problem than to the dominance-solvable guessing game the experimental literature made of it; and the higher-order-beliefs finance literature (Morris–Shin; Allen–Morris–Shin) formalizes the beauty contest without the normative layer. The three-way weave attempted in Sections 3–4 — Schelling mechanics, Heath foundations, Keynesian payoff structure — appears to be genuinely open territory, surrounded by rich adjacent literatures, mapped below.)*

### 6.1 The coordination reading of Keynes (the hinge)

- **Benjamin Wilson, "Keynes Goes Nuclear: Thomas Schelling and the Macroeconomic Origins of Strategic Stability" (*Modern Intellectual History*, 2021)** — the historical demonstration that Schelling's strategic theory descends from his Keynesian macroeconomics (see Section 2.0). The intellectual charter for this paper.
- **Axel Leijonhufvud, *On Keynesian Economics and the Economics of Keynes* (1968)** — the founding modern reinterpretation of Keynes as a theorist of inter-agent coordination failure rather than sticky wages; set the agenda every later formalization works within.
- **John Latsis, Guillemette de Larquier & Franck Bessis, "Are Conventions Solutions to Uncertainty? Contrasting Visions of Social Coordination" (*JPKE*, 2010)** — the keystone map of this whole terrain: explicitly contrasts the three developed accounts of convention — game-theoretic (Lewis–Sugden–Young), Keynesian/post-Keynesian, and French *économie des conventions* — and assesses their (surprisingly thin) overlap. The present paper is an attempt to *increase* that overlap. See also Latsis, "Is There Redemption for Conventions?" (*CJE*, 2005).
- **Jean-Pierre Dupuy, "Convention et Common Knowledge" (*Revue économique*, 1989)** — the most direct confrontation of Lewis's common-knowledge account of convention with Keynes' specular (beauty-contest) logic.
- **Jörg Bibow, Paul Lewis & Jochen Runde, "Uncertainty, Conventional Behavior, and Economic Sociology" (*AJES*, 2005)** — a rare explicit three-way bridge between Keynes' stock-market conventions, the French mimetic school, and Cambridge social ontology.
- **Shaun Hargreaves Heap, *The New Keynesian Macroeconomics: Time, Belief and Social Interdependence* (1992)** — an early, underappreciated synthesis reconstructing Keynesian macro around belief interdependence, conventions, and norms with explicitly game-theoretic tools; his *Game Theory: A Critical Introduction* (with Varoufakis) treats the beauty contest in the same spirit.
- **Alessandro Lanteri & Anna Carabelli, "Beauty Contested" (*EJHET*, 2011)** — argues the experimental p-beauty-contest tradition mis-translates Keynes: his original game is not dominance-solvable but a Schelling-style coordination problem under uncertainty. The closest existing text to this paper's Schelling–Keynes hinge.

### 6.2 The game theory of convention (the Schelling line)

- **David Lewis, *Convention* (1969)** — took Schelling's focal points and built the first rigorous theory of convention as a self-perpetuating solution to recurring coordination problems, defining common knowledge en route. The formal definition of what Keynes' Chapter 12 "convention" *is* — though Davis (1997) and Latsis et al. (2010) argue the fit is imperfect, since Keynes' convention operates where the common-knowledge basis Lewis requires is unavailable.
- **Robert Sugden, *The Economics of Rights, Co-operation and Welfare* (1986; 2nd ed. 2004)** — conventions emerge spontaneously by evolutionary dynamics and acquire *normative* force; with **Mehta, Starmer & Sugden's** experimental work on primary vs. secondary salience (*AER*, 1994) and his team-reasoning papers, Sugden is the closest thing to a one-man Schelling–Heath bridge, though he never wrote directly on Keynes.
- **Michael Bacharach, *Beyond Individual Choice* (2006, ed. Gold & Sugden)** — team reasoning: agents who frame the situation as "what should *we* do?" transform stag hunts into single-agent problems. A rival (or complement) to Heath's deontic account of how assurance problems actually get solved — and a rationality-based alternative to animal spirits as the selector of the good equilibrium.
- **Brian Skyrms, *The Stag Hunt and the Evolution of Social Structure* (2004)** — makes the stag hunt (Section 4.1's chassis) the master model of the social contract; evolutionary and signaling dynamics of the assurance problem.
- **H. Peyton Young, "The Evolution of Conventions" (*Econometrica*, 1993) and *Individual Strategy and Social Structure* (1998)** — stochastic evolution of conventions: which conventions are stochastically stable, and how societies *switch* conventions in punctuated shifts — the formal dynamics of the conventional change Keynes could only describe as "sudden and violent."
- **Ken Binmore, *Game Theory and the Social Contract* (1994/1998); *Natural Justice* (2005)** — fairness norms as equilibrium-selection devices in the game of life; a naturalistic rival to Heath on the same explanandum.
- **Cristina Bicchieri, *The Grammar of Society* (2006)** — social norms as conditional preferences keyed to empirical and normative expectations; supplies the measurement theory for ν, and the account of how norms unravel (relevant to convention collapse).
- **Cyril Hédoin** (e.g., "Community-Based Reasoning in Games," *Games*, 2016) — contemporary bridge figure connecting Lewisian convention, rule-following, and institutional economics; salience as a function of community membership (which is why σ is society-specific, as Section 5 requires).

### 6.3 Coordination-failure macroeconomics (the Keynes-formalized line)

- **John Bryant, "A Simple Rational Expectations Keynes-type Model" (*QJE*, 1983)** and **Russell Cooper & Andrew John, "Coordinating Coordination Failures in Keynesian Models" (*QJE*, 1988)** — the canonical demonstrations that strategic complementarities generate multiple Pareto-ranked macro equilibria: Keynesian unemployment as coordination on the bad equilibrium of an economy-scale stag hunt. Section 4.1 is this literature with Schelling selection and Heath stability added. Cooper's *Coordination Games* (1999) consolidates the program.
- **Peter Diamond, "Aggregate Demand Management in Search Equilibrium" (*JPE*, 1982)** — the coconut model: trading externalities generate multiple Pareto-ranked steady states; optimism about others' trading is self-fulfilling.
- **David Cass & Karl Shell, "Do Sunspots Matter?" (*JPE*, 1983)** — extrinsic uncertainty can coordinate behavior in equilibrium; a sunspot is a Schelling focal point admitted into rational-expectations theory, i.e., a convention formalized.
- **Peter Howitt & R. Preston McAfee, "Animal Spirits" (*AER*, 1992)** — self-fulfilling waves of optimism and pessimism switch a search economy between high- and low-activity equilibria under fully rational expectations: Keynes' animal spirits recast as an equilibrium-selection variable.
- **Roger Farmer, *The Macroeconomics of Self-Fulfilling Prophecies* (1993); *Expectations, Employment and Prices* (2010); *Prosperity for All* (2016)** — beliefs as an independent fundamental; the "belief function" supplies the missing equilibrium-selection equation, and a continuum of unemployment steady states replaces the natural rate. The closest thing modern macro has to a formal Chapter 12.
- **Stephen Morris & Hyun Song Shin, "Social Value of Public Information" (*AER*, 2002)** and **Franklin Allen, Morris & Shin, "Beauty Contests and Iterated Expectations in Asset Markets" (*RFS*, 2006)** — the rationalist vindication of the beauty contest: with higher-order beliefs, the law of iterated expectations fails for average opinion, prices overweight public signals *because* they are focal, and public information is powerful for Schelling reasons, not informational ones. Their global-games program (Morris–Shin, *AER*, 1998) turns multiple-equilibrium crisis stories into a theory of *when* conventions break.
- **Van Huyck, Battalio & Beil, "Tacit Coordination Games, Strategic Uncertainty, and Coordination Failure" (*AER*, 1990)** — the canonical experiments: real groups playing stag-hunt-like games reliably converge on inefficient, secure equilibria as group size grows. The laboratory counterpart of involuntary unemployment — and of why large anonymous economies (low ν) coordinate worse than small thick ones.
- **Rosemarie Nagel, "Unraveling in Guessing Games" (*AER*, 1995)**, **Bosch-Domènech, Montalvo, Nagel & Satorra (*AER*, 2002)**, **Camerer, *Behavioral Game Theory* (2003)**, and **Mauersberger & Nagel's** *Handbook of Computational Economics* chapter (2018) — Keynes' guessing game made operational; level-k reasoning as measured, finite-depth iteration of "average opinion about average opinion," run in labs and in actual newspapers; the 2018 chapter argues formally that New Keynesian expectations models have beauty-contest structure.
- **George Akerlof & Robert Shiller, *Animal Spirits* (2009)** — the behavioral-macro restatement: confidence, fairness, corruption, money illusion, and *stories* as macro forces. Shiller's *Narrative Economics* (2019) is σ studied as epidemiology. **Marchionatti (*Kyklos*, 1999)** disciplines the appropriation: in Keynes, animal spirits are non-rational but *reasonable* conduct under uncertainty, not a bias.
- **Roman Frydman & Michael Goldberg, *Imperfect Knowledge Economics* (2007)**; **John Kay & Mervyn King, *Radical Uncertainty* (2020)** — the modern restatements of Keynesian uncertainty against probabilistic reduction; Kay and King's "reference narratives" as devices for coping and coordinating are Schelling σ under another name.

### 6.4 Keynes scholarship on convention and uncertainty (the philology)

- **Anna Carabelli, *On Keynes's Method* (1988)**; **Rod O'Donnell, *Keynes: Philosophy, Economics and Politics* (1989)**; **John B. Davis, *Keynes's Philosophical Development* (1994)** and **"J. M. Keynes on History and Convention" (1997)**; **Sheila Dow** (with Alexander Dow, "Animal Spirits and Rationality," 1985); **Victoria Chick, *Macroeconomics after Keynes* (1983)**; **Tony Lawson, "Keynes and Conventions" (*Review of Social Economy*, 1993)** — establish from the *Treatise on Probability* through the 1937 QJE restatement that convention, weight of argument, and non-numerical probability are the *philosophical core* of Keynes, not local color. Davis is especially important for this paper: he argues the later Keynes held an irreducibly *social*, interdependent conception of judgment — and warns where Lewis-style equilibrium readings of Keynesian convention overreach.
- **Jochen Runde & Sohei Mizuhara (eds.), *The Philosophy of Keynes' Economics: Probability, Uncertainty and Convention* (2003)** — the central edited volume on this intersection's philosophical side, with a dedicated section on convention.
- **David Dequech** — "Financial Conventions in Keynes's Theory: The Stock Exchange" (*JPKE*, 2011), "Conventions in Keynes's Theory of Goods Markets" (*JPKE*, 2022), and related papers: the most systematic contemporary taxonomist of Keynesian conventions, distinguishing conformity mechanisms and relating conventions to institutions and to the game-theoretic literature. The nearest existing analogue of this paper's ν parameter.
- **The French *économie des conventions* school** — founded in the 1989 *Revue économique* special issue (Dupuy, Eymard-Duvernay, Favereau, Orléan, Salais, Thévenot), with Keynes' convention explicitly as its point of departure. **Olivier Favereau** ("La Théorie Générale: de l'économie conventionnelle à l'économie des conventions," 1988; "Keynes After the Economics of Conventions," 2013) calls Keynes "the first and greatest conventionalist economist" and reads both 1929 and 2008 as failures of twin conventions. **André Orléan** ("Mimetic Contagion and Speculative Bubbles," 1989; *The Empire of Value*, 2014) generalizes the beauty contest into a full theory of economic value: liquidity requires a "convention of valuation" produced by mimetic polarization, not discovery of fundamentals. **Boltanski & Thévenot, *On Justification* (1991/2006)** supply the school's sociological wing — plural "orders of worth" as competing conventions of evaluation, useful for Section 5's claim that different societies justify commitment differently.
- **Jens Beckert, *Imagined Futures: Fictional Expectations and Capitalist Dynamics* (2016)** — "fictional expectations" as the sociology of Chapter 12: under fundamental uncertainty, coordination runs on shared imaginaries of the future, which are politically produced and contested.

### 6.5 Cooperation, norms, and the moral economy (the Heath line)

- **Samuel Bowles & Herbert Gintis, *A Cooperative Species* (2011)**; **Ernst Fehr and colleagues** (Fehr & Gächter, *AER*, 2000; Fischbacher, Gächter & Fehr, *Economics Letters*, 2001) — the empirical human is a *conditional* cooperator with a taste for norm enforcement: people cooperate if they expect others to. Aggregate cooperation therefore has beauty-contest structure itself — which is the experimental basis for ν, and why Withdraw cascades once norm violation becomes visible (conditional cooperation running in reverse is a bank run).
- **Elinor Ostrom, *Governing the Commons* (1990)** — self-organized assurance without the state; the boundary conditions (small scale, monitoring, graduated sanctions) that explain why *macro* assurance, which violates all of them, needs the state.
- **George Akerlof** (fair-wage/effort models; *Identity Economics* with Kranton, 2010) and **Truman Bewley, *Why Wages Don't Fall During a Recession* (1999)** — the Heathian reading of wage stickiness (Section 3.1) documented in the field.
- **Albert Hirschman, *Exit, Voice, and Loyalty* (1970)** — λ is exit; ν is loyalty; the trade-off between them is Hirschman's, and his *The Passions and the Interests* (1977) is the deep history of Heath's efficiency defense of cooperative capitalism.

### 6.6 Comparative institutional calibration (the Section 5 toolkit)

- **Peter Hall & David Soskice, *Varieties of Capitalism* (2001)** — LME/CME as (explicitly game-theoretic) national solutions to coordination problems; the US/Japan contrast is their paradigm case. For China, which the binary cannot hold, see **Witt & Redding's** business-systems mapping (*Socio-Economic Review*, 2013) and **Nölke, ten Brink, May & Claar, *State-Permeated Capitalism in Large Emerging Economies* (2019)**.
- **Masahiko Aoki, *Toward a Comparative Institutional Analysis* (2001)** — institutions as self-sustaining systems of shared beliefs, i.e., equilibrium summaries of societal games; institutional complementarities generate multiple viable national configurations. The formal bridge between Schelling conventions and national systems, built by the leading theorist of the Japanese firm.
- **Avner Greif, "Cultural Beliefs and the Organization of Society" (*JPE*, 1994) and *Institutions and the Path to the Modern Economy* (2006)** — collectivist vs. individualist equilibria (Maghribi/Genoese) as durable cultural conventions of enforcement; the historical demonstration that shared expectations determine *which economy you get*, and the foundation for the China/US ν contrast.
- **Francis Fukuyama, *Trust* (1995)**; **Joseph Henrich, *The WEIRDest People in the World* (2020)**; **Michele Gelfand, *Rule Makers, Rule Breakers* (2018)** — the trust-radius, kinship-psychology, and tightness-looseness measurements underlying the four-country parameterization.
- **Richard Koo, *The Holy Grail of Macroeconomics* (2008)** — balance-sheet recession: Japan's private sector minimizing debt rather than maximizing profit is the Withdraw equilibrium documented from flow-of-funds data; Koo is also the standard lens now applied to post-2021 China.

## 7. Coda: what the weave changes

Three conclusions, in ascending order of ambition.

**For reading Keynes.** The *General Theory* rewards being read as an early and remarkably complete description of a monetary stag hunt: an economy whose multiple equilibria are created by liquidity (Ch. 17), selected by convention and salience (Ch. 12), propagated by coupling (the multiplier), stabilized by norms (Ch. 2's wage conventions), and governable by the one agent plausibly capable of commitment and risk pooling at scale (Ch. 24). Much of the machinery Keynes lacked in 1936 now exists: Schelling built the selection and dynamics, Heath built the stability and the normative accounting, and the coordination-failure literature built the formal chassis. What has not been done — and what this paper sketches — is assembling them in one frame. The historical warrant is stronger than generally noticed: Schelling's game theory was Keynesian macrodynamics exported to strategy (Wilson 2021); bringing it back to macroeconomics is repatriation, not appropriation.

**For macroeconomic policy.** "Confidence" decomposes into three separately manipulable objects — salience (σ), normative thickness (ν), and exit pull (λ) — and the four-country calibration shows that societies differ not in whether they solve the assurance problem but in *which instrument they have historically leaned on*, and that each society's characteristic crisis is the failure mode of its own favorite instrument. Policy advice that ignores this is exporting a solution to a problem the importing society doesn't have: telling 1990s Japan to cut rates (its problem was ν-lock-in, not λ-pricing), or telling 2020s China to cut rates (its problem is a σ-rupture plus a λ rooted in the risk-pooling gap), repeats the same category error. The general prescription is Tinbergen for coordination: match the instrument to the broken parameter.

**For political philosophy.** Heath's program and Keynes' converge on a single claim from opposite directions: on this reading, the market order's deepest vulnerability is not injustice but *assurance failure*, and the institutions that remedy it — the welfare state as risk pool, the central bank as focal point, financial regulation as norm-thickener, public investment as standing commitment — are justified on efficiency grounds internal to the market's own logic, needing no appeal beyond it. That is why the weave holds: Schelling explains how strangers coordinate, Keynes explains why a monetary economy of strangers can coordinate on disaster, and Heath explains why the repair of that failure is not a departure from the cooperative order but its completion.

A research agenda follows naturally: formalize the (σ, ν, λ) stag hunt and derive the comparative statics of Section 5.5 (Section 8 examines what that exercise would put at risk, and Appendix B carries out a first round of it); operationalize ν behaviorally (Bicchieri-style norm measurement inside financial and wage-setting practices, extending Dequech's taxonomy); test the "casino conjecture" (conventional-revision violence as a function of a market's normative thinness) across the four financial systems; and run the beauty-contest experiments cross-culturally with Gelfand tightness as a treatment variable — the lab analogue of the calibration attempted here.

## 8. What formalization would put at risk

The case for formalizing the (σ, ν, λ) stag hunt is not that it would vindicate the model but that it is the most reliable way to discover the model's limits. A verbal model with three Greek letters is dangerously flexible: any episode can be narrated after the fact as "σ fell" or "ν eroded," and Section 5's calibration, however disciplined, cannot fully escape that flexibility. Writing the game down attacks it in four ways.

**Formalization forces the parameters to become definite objects — and may show there are not three of them.** At present σ, ν, and λ are placeholders. To write payoffs one must decide: is σ a public signal, a correlation device, a prior over others' play? Is ν a term in the utility function, a lexical constraint, or a cost of switching strategies? Each choice surfaces hidden problems. The most important: if ν is modeled as a payoff term ("norm-violation is costly"), it collapses into the payoff matrix and becomes observationally equivalent to ordinary preferences — the standard unfalsifiability critique of norm-based explanations, which the verbal model inherits silently. And σ and ν may not be independent: a focal point's authority might just *be* the thickness of the practice that produces it. Formalization is how one finds out whether the model has three parameters, or two, or one.

**Formalization reveals that the model's predictions depend on which mathematics is chosen — which is itself a discovered limit.** The candidate machineries give different answers. A global-games formalization (Carlsson–van Damme; Morris–Shin) tends to deliver a *unique* equilibrium — dissolving the multiplicity on which the whole construction rests. Stochastic-stability dynamics (Kandori–Mailath–Rob; Young) restore multiplicity but predict which convention prevails in the long run, with transitions driven by accumulated noise rather than by common-knowledge cliff events — in tension with Section 4.5's discontinuity claim. An epistemic formalization runs into the difficulty Davis flagged: focal points are hard to define where common knowledge fails, yet the model claims conventions matter most there. If several reasonable formalizations disagree, the verbal model underdetermines its own predictions — a limit no quantity of country narrative would have exposed.

**Formalization makes the comparative claims checkable rather than narrated.** "Thick ν delays collapse but entrenches bad equilibria" (the Japan thesis) sounds as if it follows, but does it? Formally it requires an asymmetry assumption — that norms defend whatever convention is *incumbent*, good or bad — and one can check whether that is a theorem, a knife-edge, or false. The same applies to the substitution claim (σ, ν, λ as partial substitutes) and to the China prescription (lowering λ beats repairing σ). Some of these will likely fail or acquire conditions, and the conditions are the model's limits, stated where they bind.

**Formalization exposes identification limits for the calibration.** Once the mapping from (σ, ν, λ) to observable behavior is explicit, one can ask whether the four-country evidence could distinguish Section 5's parameterization from rivals. The suspicion must be that the current evidence is consistent with many configurations; a formal model would say which observations *would* discriminate — converting the calibration from illustration toward test design.

One caution, supplied by this paper's own literature review. Formalization discovers the limits of the *formalization*, not automatically of the idea. Favereau's charge against the neoclassical synthesis was that it formalized Keynes by replacing radical uncertainty with something tractable, and lost the core in the mapping; the same failure mode is available here — a global-games treatment "formalizes" the beauty contest by reintroducing the calculable priors Keynes denied. The protection is plurality: build deliberately different formal versions and read their *disagreements* as information about which features of the verbal model are load-bearing and which were artifacts of prose. Appendix B carries out a first round of this program: two formalizations — a global game with higher-order beliefs, and an evolutionary model with stochastically stable conventions — followed by an analysis of where they disagree with each other and with the verbal model (B.3), and a survey of the candidate mathematics for the open problem that analysis uncovers (B.4).

## Appendix B. Two formalizations, and where they disagree

*Section 8 argued that formalization is a limit-discovery exercise and that its value comes from plurality: build deliberately different formal versions and read their disagreements as information. This appendix carries out a first round. B.1 formalizes the (σ, ν, λ) stag hunt as a global game with higher-order beliefs (Carlsson–van Damme; Morris–Shin); B.2 as an evolutionary model with stochastically stable conventions (Kandori–Mailath–Rob; Young). B.3 works out the disagreements — between the two formalizations, and between each of them and the verbal model — and ends at the program's deepest open problem: neither formalism can host Keynes' conventional judgment, which is forward-looking but non-calculable. B.4 surveys the candidate mathematics that might. The formal sketches state results with one-line arguments or pointers to known theorems, not proofs.*

### B.1 Formalization I: a global game

**Setup.** A continuum of agents $i \in [0,1]$ simultaneously choose $s_i \in \{C, W\}$ (Commit / Withdraw). Let $m$ be the mass choosing $C$, $\theta$ an underlying fundamental (profitability of commitment gross of demand externalities), and $\kappa \in \{C, W\}$ the **incumbent convention** — a state variable inherited from the past: the action "everyone knows" one is supposed to take. Payoffs:

$$u(C \mid m, \theta, \kappa) = \theta + a\,m - c\,\mathbf{1}\{\kappa = W\}, \qquad u(W \mid \kappa) = \lambda - c\,\mathbf{1}\{\kappa = C\},$$

with $a > 0$ the demand complementarity and $c \ge 0$ a deontic cost of playing *contrary to* the incumbent convention. Under complete information both pure conventions are strict equilibria for $\theta$ in an intermediate band, Pareto-ranked whenever $\theta + a > \lambda$: all-$W$ is "unemployment." Information: $\theta \sim \mathcal{N}(z, \alpha_0^{-1})$; private signals $x_i = \theta + \varepsilon_i$ with precision $\beta$; a public signal $y = \theta + \eta$ with precision $\alpha$ (prior folded in). The parameter mapping: **σ = α** (the public signal is the one object on which higher-order beliefs can concentrate); **ν = c** (an asymmetric switching cost defending whichever convention is incumbent); **λ = the safe payoff** itself.

Three mapping failures, reported rather than papered over. (a) σ conflates *precision*, *publicity* (a structural feature, not a dial), and Schelling *focality* — prominence and legitimacy, which need not correlate with informational accuracy at all; a salient-but-uninformative signal has $\alpha \approx 0$ yet real focal points (round numbers, incumbency) plainly coordinate. (b) ν as a payoff cost preserves full Bayesian recalculation and merely tilts it — whereas the verbal model's ν means commitment is *not continuously recalculated*; an alternative mapping (a fraction of automaton agents who replay κ regardless of signals) captures that and is not equivalent. (c) λ as a constant understates Keynesian liquidity preference, which is an option value that should covary with confidence — making σ and λ not truly independent primitives in the intended interpretation.

**Equilibrium.** In monotone strategies (commit iff $x_i \ge \hat{x}$), the marginal agent's indifference condition yields a threshold $\theta^{\ast}(y, \kappa)$. The Morris–Shin condition for uniqueness across all realizations of $y$ is, in sufficient form,

$$\frac{\alpha}{\sqrt{\beta}} \;\le\; \frac{\sqrt{2\pi}}{a},$$

i.e., private views must be dispersed enough to break the common-knowledge scaffolding on which multiple self-fulfilling conventions rest. Note that $c$ enters only additively: **ν relocates the threshold but does not affect uniqueness.** In the canonical limit $\beta \to \infty$, the marginal agent holds the Laplacian (uniform) belief over $m$ and the cutoffs collapse to

$$\theta^{\ast}_{\kappa = C} = \lambda - \tfrac{a}{2} - c, \qquad \theta^{\ast}_{\kappa = W} = \lambda - \tfrac{a}{2} + c.$$

With $c = 0$ this is risk-dominance selection (Carlsson–van Damme): the economy commits iff $\theta > \lambda - a/2$, *not* iff commitment is Pareto-superior ($\theta > \lambda - a$). So on $\theta \in (\lambda - a, \lambda - a/2)$ the economy sits in the bad regime at fundamentals where the good regime exists and is better: **coordination failure survives equilibrium uniqueness** — a genuinely Keynesian output of the formalism, and one the verbal model did not anticipate. With $c > 0$ there is a **hysteresis band** of width $2c$ on which the incumbent convention persists whichever it is.

**Comparative statics.** $\partial\theta^{\ast}/\partial\lambda = +1$: the cleanest lever. $\partial/\partial c$: widens the hysteresis band symmetrically — but only under the assumption that the deviation cost is the same whichever action is prescribed ($c_C = c_W$); the Japan-lock-in reading requires that "hoard and wait" itself becomes norm-governed, a substantive and checkable assumption, not a freebie. And if $c$ erodes in the mass of deviators, the norm term itself becomes a complementarity that can violate the uniqueness condition: thick-but-brittle norms generate cliffs. $\partial/\partial\alpha$: two-edged. An authoritative optimistic signal lowers the effective hurdle; but because $y$ forecasts *others' beliefs* and not just $\theta$, equilibrium behavior loads on $y$ beyond its Bayesian weight (Morris–Shin 2002), so **raising salience can raise volatility** — announcement noise is amplified into regime risk, and public information of low relative precision can be socially harmful. The verbal model's "salience supplies assurance" becomes a sign-ambiguous statement.

**Where multiplicity lives.** As $\alpha \to \infty$ the game converges to the complete-information game and full multiplicity returns: *precise public information approximately reconstitutes common knowledge — the very thing whose absence delivered uniqueness*. And with endogenous public signals (prices, observed aggregate activity: Angeletos–Werning 2006), uniqueness is non-robust in the settings the verbal model most cares about. So claim (i) of Section 4 — multiple Pareto-ranked equilibria — survives, but as a *regime* of the model (the high-σ, endogenous-information regime), not a global property; and it survives *because of* the mechanism the verbal model calls salience.

**Discontinuity.** Aggregate behavior $m(\theta)$ is continuous at finite precision; discrete regime outcomes jump when $\theta$ crosses $\theta^{\ast}$ — cliff events exist, but they are *fundamentals-crossing-a-threshold* events. The verbal model's epistemic cascade ("loss of common knowledge becomes common knowledge") is untranslatable in the uniqueness region, where there is never common knowledge to lose; the nearest formal homes for it are (a) public-noise shocks — a bad draw of $\eta$ flipping the regime with real fundamentals unchanged, a collapse of shared confidence operating through the higher-order role of $y$ — and (b) the multiplicity region, where genuinely self-fulfilling sunspot cliffs return.

**Policy.** σ-policy coordinates assurance but is a volatility machine when inaccurate, and at very high α re-opens multiplicity, making the authority hostage to self-fulfilling attacks on its own credibility. ν-policy widens the hysteresis band: insurance ex ante, obstacle to recovery ex post — the value of norm-thickening is regime-contingent while norms are, by nature, non-contingent; a "convention-breaking" policy (temporarily subsidizing deviation from $\kappa = W$) is the mirror lever. λ-policy shifts the threshold one-for-one, works in both regions, and requires no credibility about beliefs; in the hysteresis band, a *temporary* λ cut large enough to exit the band on the good side flips the convention *permanently*, after which ν defends the good regime — a formal version of "pump-priming works by resetting the convention, not by the spending itself."

**Strains and losses.** (1) Knightian uncertainty is replaced by a common prior: higher-order beliefs become calculable, so the convention is derived from probabilities — when Keynes' point was that the convention exists *because* no such probabilities are available. The formalization solves the problem by assuming away the condition that generated it. (2) Focality is reduced to signal precision; authority as normative deference is not representable — agents obey Bayes, not the central bank. (3) ν as a payoff term is observationally equivalent to a switching-cost preference; Heath's distinction between norm-following as a non-calculative *mode* of action and preference-maximization is erased by construction. (4) Deepest: the machinery needs exogenous dominance regions — states where one action is dominant regardless of others — but on the verbal model's own account the profitability of commitment is constituted by aggregate demand itself. Push that reading to its limit and the dominance regions vanish and the entire selection apparatus fails. Scope condition, to be stated rather than hidden: *the global game selects among conventions only where fundamentals retain independent bite.* (5) What transfers intact: the Pareto-ranking of regimes — indeed strengthened, since inefficiency no longer requires multiplicity at all.

### B.2 Formalization II: evolutionary dynamics and stochastic stability

**Setup.** A population of $N$ agents, each holding an action in $\{C, W\}$. Stage game (random-matching stag hunt; the $N$-player threshold version behaves analogously): $C$ against $C$ pays $A$; $C$ against $W$ pays $B$; $W$ pays $\lambda$ regardless, with $B < \lambda < A$. Both monomorphic states are strict equilibria — conventions — and all-$C$ Pareto-dominates: claim (i) of Section 4 is delivered at the stage-game level. Dynamics (Young's adaptive play): each period a revising agent samples $s$ of the last $m$ periods of play and myopically best-responds to the sampled frequency $\hat p$ of $C$; with probability $\varepsilon$ she instead experiments at random. As $\varepsilon \to 0$, the process spends almost all time at the **stochastically stable** convention.

The parameter mapping. **λ = the safe payoff**, the only stage-game parameter that moves the best-response threshold. **ν has two candidate mappings that turn out to behave differently** (a substantive discovery, not a nuisance): (ν-a) a *deontic term* $d(\nu)$ rewarding conformity with the **incumbent** convention $\bar x$ — whichever it is; (ν-b) *inertia* — norm-governed agents revise less often and tremble less. **σ likewise has two**: (σ-a) a *tie-breaker/prior* — a public prescription $z$ agents condition on when samples leave them indifferent; (σ-b) a *correlated re-coordination device* — announcements arrive at rate $\eta$, each agent hears-and-trusts with probability $\mu(\sigma)$, and hearers who expect enough followers switch to $z$ simultaneously.

**Basins.** Commit is a best response iff $\hat p \ge q^{\ast} = \frac{\lambda - B}{A - B}$ — the **assurance threshold**, increasing in λ. Under (ν-a) the incumbent's basin widens by $d/(A-B)$ *at the challenger's expense*, whichever convention is incumbent: normative thickness is a moat around whatever currently reigns, not around the good regime.

**Stochastic stability: Keynesian pessimism as a theorem.** The transition resistances are $r(W \to C) = \lceil s\,\frac{\lambda - B + d}{A-B} \rceil$ and $r(C \to W) = \lceil s\,\frac{A - \lambda + d}{A-B} \rceil$, and the stochastically stable convention is the one harder to leave (KMR 1993; Young 1993): all-$C$ wins iff $\lambda < \frac{A+B}{2}$ — the **risk-dominance** condition. For $\frac{A+B}{2} < \lambda < A$, Commit is payoff-dominant but Withdraw is risk-dominant, and the theorem says the economy spends almost all of the long run in the unemployment convention: the bad equilibrium is selected not because anyone prefers it but because it is the safer thing to be wrong about — the loss from committing alone ($\lambda - B$) exceeds the loss from hoarding through a boom ($A - \lambda$). Liquidity preference wins the evolutionary contest when the penalty for unreciprocated commitment is large. Note two sharp results: the incumbent-deontic $d$ **cancels** from the selection comparison (it adds equally to both resistances), so under (ν-a) normative thickness affects *persistence*, never *selection*; whereas a content-laden pro-Commit norm $d^C$ ("the honorable man invests") does shift selection. And uniform reductions in $\varepsilon$ (mapping ν-b) leave selection unchanged — though Bergin–Lipman (1996) warn that *state-dependent* tremble rates can engineer any selection whatever, so ν-b is a powerful and dangerous modeling degree of freedom.

**Transition dynamics.** Waiting times between conventions are of order $\varepsilon^{-r}$ — exponential in the sampling scale — while the transition itself, once triggered, completes at best-response speed: the **punctuated-equilibrium** signature. Ex post, the transition is a cliff. But the mechanism is accumulated idiosyncratic noise crossing a basin boundary: **no epistemics at all** — no beliefs about beliefs, no knowledge cascades. The model reproduces the *phenomenology* of claim (ii) while replacing its mechanism, showing that cliff-shaped transitions do not by themselves evidence common-knowledge dynamics. (Ellison 1993: local interaction restores plausible transition speeds via contagion from small clusters, but selection is still risk dominance — localism cannot rescue the good equilibrium.) On claim (iii): under (ν-a) the entrenchment is symmetric — the same $\nu$ that sustains the high-commitment convention produces Japan-style hysteresis when all-$W$ is incumbent, because the deontic cost of breaking ranks deters the first movers a recovery needs. The formal distinction between incumbent-attached and content-laden norms is easy to conflate verbally and impossible to conflate formally.

**σ as re-coordination device.** Under (σ-b), an announcement of $C$ from the all-$W$ state succeeds — "all hearers follow" is an equilibrium of the announcement subgame — iff

$$\mu(\sigma) \;\ge\; \frac{\lambda - B + d(\nu)}{A - B} \qquad \text{(CR, the credibility constraint)}.$$

Three lessons. (1) "Nobody follows" is *always* also an equilibrium of the announcement subgame: the announcement creates a coordination problem one level up, and σ's substantive content is that it makes the follow-equilibrium focal — Schelling reappears *inside* the formal model rather than being assumed away; (CR) is authority's capacity constraint, not a free pass. (2) (CR) displays claim (iv) as an explicit frontier — $\mu(\sigma)(A-B) + B \ge \lambda + d(\nu)$ — with the interaction that **thick ν raises the bar on σ**: norm-bound societies are harder to talk out of a bad convention. (3) When (CR) holds, announcements convert an exponentially long noise-driven wait into an $O(\eta^{-1})$ jump, making the payoff-dominant convention the effective long-run state even when it is not risk-dominant — the formal version of "animal spirits can be governed." By contrast, the tie-breaker mapping (σ-a) is nearly inert at conventions (unanimous samples never reach the tie-breaking event): as a mild persuader σ is epiphenomenal; as a correlation device it is the most powerful parameter in the model.

**Policy corollary — sequencing.** Because incumbent-attached ν entrenches whatever reigns, the model prescribes: *coordinate first (σ), thicken second (ν)*. Raising ν during a slump cements the slump and raises the σ needed to escape it; raising ν after successful re-coordination buys exponential stability against the fact that Withdraw may still be risk-dominant. This sequencing result is invisible in the verbal model and is arguably the formal core of its policy story.

**Strains and losses.** (1) **Expectation is annihilated, not simplified**: agents are myopic backward-looking samplers with no view of the future at all. Keynes' state of long-term expectation has no representative — arguably a worse distortion than rational expectations, which at least models forward-looking belief (falsely disciplined). Young's convention (a stable pattern of *play*) is a pale shadow of Keynes' (a stable pattern of *judgment about the future*). (2) Uncertainty is trivialized into sampling noise and i.i.d. trembles; nothing corresponds to "we simply do not know." (3) Higher-order beliefs have no home: the beauty contest is structurally absent. (4) Mistake-accumulation is macroscopically implausible: escaping a convention needs $\Theta(N)$ same-direction idiosyncratic errors, so waiting times at economy scale are cosmological; the mechanism is literal only for small groups (a policy elite, a financial community) or must be re-founded on aggregate shocks or local interaction. Relatedly, uniform-$\varepsilon$ selection is knife-edge (Blume's logit noise, Bergin–Lipman's state-dependence can overturn it): "pessimism as a theorem" is a theorem *about a particular noise model*. (5) Timescale calibration fails: evolutionary transitions are calibrated to conventions-of-centuries, business cycles to years; the σ-jump channel rescues the clock but is itself (6) a deus ex machina — $\mu(\sigma)$ is unmodeled Schelling-focality by fiat: the model formalizes *when* an announcement can work, not *why* salience produces convergent expectations. (7) Payoffs are stationary (no paradox-of-thrift feedback making λ endogenous to aggregate withdrawal), agents homogeneous (no entrepreneur/rentier distinction), and the economy's web of interlocking conventions is reduced to one binary coordinate.

### B.3 Working out the disagreements

The exercise pays off in both directions: where the two formalizations *agree* against the verbal model, the verbal model has a real problem; where they *disagree with each other*, the verbal model's vocabulary has been shown to conflate distinct objects. Taking Section 4.5's claims in turn, with the verdicts tabulated at the end.

**On multiplicity (claim i), the two mathematics disagree — and the disagreement redraws the claim.** The global game makes multiplicity an *information-regime property*: unique equilibrium under dispersed private information, multiplicity restored where public information is precise or endogenous (prices, observed activity). The evolutionary model has stage-game multiplicity always, but its long-run selection theorem makes the multiplicity *practically* moot: one convention absorbs almost all of the long run. Neither delivers the verbal model's picture of an economy freely parked at either of two equilibria. Yet both converge on something better: **coordination failure does not need multiplicity.** In the global game the unique equilibrium is inefficient over a whole interval of fundamentals; in the evolutionary model the selected convention is the inefficient one whenever Withdraw is risk-dominant. The verbal model's deepest commitment — involuntary unemployment as a stable, self-enforcing social outcome rather than a friction — survives in both formalizations *more* robustly than the multiplicity rhetoric that carried it.

**On selection, the two mathematics agree, by different routes — the strongest convergence result of the exercise.** The global game's Laplacian limit and the evolutionary model's resistance counting both select the **risk-dominant** convention: commitment prevails only if $\lambda < \lambda^{crit}$ (with $\lambda^{crit} = \theta + a/2$ in one notation, $(A+B)/2$ in the other), not merely if commitment is better for everyone. Two unrelated formal machineries, one built on hyper-sophisticated higher-order Bayesians and the other on myopic samplers with no beliefs at all, arrive at the same inequality. That convergence is evidence the result reflects the payoff structure rather than the machinery — with one qualifier carried over from B.2: the evolutionary half of the convergence holds for the canonical uniform-tremble noise model, and Bergin–Lipman-style state-dependent noise can overturn it, so the agreement is between the *canonical* versions of the two machineries, not between the families in full generality. So qualified, it still hands the paper a sharpened central thesis: *the monetary economy's tragedy is that safety is judged by unilateral deviation losses while prosperity requires joint commitment; the gap between risk dominance and payoff dominance is the formal size of the Keynesian problem.*

**On discontinuity (claim ii), both mathematics reject the verbal mechanism while reproducing its phenomenology — the clearest discovered limit.** The verbal model says cliffs happen when "loss of common knowledge becomes common knowledge." The global game replies that in its uniqueness region there is never common knowledge to lose, and cliffs are threshold-crossings by fundamentals or public noise; the evolutionary model produces sharp cliffs from accumulated idiosyncratic noise with no epistemic content whatever. Since both reproduce the observable shape (long stability, sudden break) from non-epistemic mechanisms, **the cliff shape of crises cannot be cited as evidence for the epistemic story** — the verbal model's mechanism is underdetermined by macro data alone. The claim does not die: the global game's public-noise channel (a bad draw of the public signal flipping the regime with fundamentals unchanged) and its high-σ multiplicity region are formal homes for confidence collapse. But discriminating between mechanisms now demands belief data — measured higher-order expectations around crisis dates — not output data. Section 7's research agenda acquires a sharp new item.

**On normative thickness (claim iii), the two mathematics agree twice over — a conditional vindication and an identification warning.** Both find that ν, modeled as attaching to the *incumbent* convention, entrenches good and bad regimes symmetrically: a hysteresis band of width $2c$ in one model, equal additions to both escape resistances in the other. The Japan thesis is therefore a *conditional theorem*: it holds if and only if the norm defends incumbency as such — if "hoard and pay down debt" itself became the prescribed behavior, which is a substantive, checkable claim about post-bubble Japan rather than a modeling freebie. Both models also flag the same alternative — a *content-laden* norm (pro-commitment as such) behaves differently, shifting selection rather than persistence — and the same equivalence problem: ν as a payoff term cannot be distinguished from a switching-cost preference by choice data alone. The verbal model's single ν is thus formally two objects (incumbent-attached vs. content-laden), and its measurement program must target the *mode* of reasoning (Bicchieri-style norm elicitation), not behavior.

**On salience (claim iv's engine), the two mathematics disagree most sharply — and the disagreement dissects σ.** The global game renders σ as the *precision of public information* and finds it two-edged: more salience means tighter coordination but also amplified overreaction to public noise, and at high precision the return of multiplicity — authority becomes hostage to self-fulfilling attacks on itself. The evolutionary model renders σ as a *correlation device* and finds it either nearly inert (as a tie-breaker) or the single most powerful parameter in the model (as a re-coordination technology), with no informational downside because it carries no information. These are not rival estimates of one parameter; they are formalizations of *different components* of Schelling's focal point — informational authority versus pure coordinating prominence — which the verbal σ ran together. Notably, both components arrive at the same political economy: **credibility cannot be self-supplied.** In the global game, high-σ authority invites attack on its own signal; in the evolutionary model, "nobody follows" is always an equilibrium of the announcement subgame and (CR) is a capacity constraint. Both formalizations thus independently derive the Section 5.4 China result — the σ-monopolist cannot re-inflate a convention it shot — as a theorem schema rather than a narrative.

**On λ and the policy mapping (claim v), the mathematics agree comfortably.** Both rank λ as the cleanest, least belief-dependent lever (one-for-one threshold shifts; the only stage-game parameter entering risk dominance), both derive a version of the temporary-becomes-permanent result (a λ cut that exits the hysteresis band, or a σ-jump that crosses the basin, is then defended by the very ν that resisted it), and the evolutionary model adds a sequencing theorem the verbal model lacked: *coordinate first, thicken second* — with its corollary that thick ν raises the σ needed for escape (norm-bound societies are harder to talk out of a slump: Japan, quantified). The China prescription of Section 5.4 — lower λ structurally via risk pooling rather than repair σ — is consistent with both models, with the evolutionary model adding the caveat that λ relief is regime-decisive only when it crosses the risk-dominance threshold.

**What both formalizations lost, in complementary directions — the deepest limit found.** The global game keeps forward-looking belief but makes it calculable from a common prior; the evolutionary model keeps history and precedent but abolishes forward-looking belief entirely. Keynes' conventional judgment — forward-looking but *not* calculable, structured but not probabilistic — falls between the two formalisms, captured by neither. This is the discovered boundary of the whole exercise: the available mathematics offers a fork (calculable beliefs, or no beliefs), and the *General Theory*'s central object is the refusal of both prongs. Identifying mathematics that can host conventional judgment without collapsing into either prong is, on this evidence, the most important open problem the formalization program has produced. B.4 surveys the candidates.

### B.4 Candidate mathematics for conventional judgment

B.3 ended at a fork: the global game keeps forward-looking belief but makes it calculable; the evolutionary model keeps precedent but abolishes belief. The *General Theory*'s central object — conventional judgment, forward-looking but non-calculable — refuses both prongs. This subsection surveys the mathematics that might host it. The test for each candidate is fourfold: keep beliefs forward-looking; avoid reducing them to a calculation from a unique prior; leave a structural role for convention as the thing that fills the resulting gap; and remain tractable enough to yield comparative statics. Four families qualify for serious consideration.

**Family 1: beliefs without unique priors — ambiguity and imprecise probability.** In multiple-priors (maxmin) expected utility (Gilboa–Schmeidler 1989), the agent holds a *set* of priors rather than one. The Keynesian payoff arrives in equilibrium: Epstein and Wang (1994) show that ambiguity generates *equilibrium price indeterminacy* — the market's own logic fails to pin the price, and something extra-logical must. Epstein and Wang themselves note that the gap could be filled by "conventions." That is, structurally, the Chapter 12 claim: valuation is underdetermined by fundamentals, so convention selects. Kajii and Ui extend multiple priors to incomplete-information games, weakening the global-game uniqueness results from within. Adjacent: Dempster–Shafer belief functions (Shafer 1976) allow belief mass that need not sum to one, giving a formal home to Keynes' "weight of argument" from the *Treatise on Probability* — low weight as uncommitted belief mass — with the convention cast as the *completion rule* converting partial belief into action; and Hansen–Sargent robustness gives agents who distrust their own model. The family's caveat: maxmin is still a calculation (pessimization over the set), and the prior set is exogenous. But note what that exogeneity does — it *relocates* the non-calculable element into an explicit slot rather than eliminating it. That observation becomes the criterion below.

**Family 2: beliefs disciplined only by experience, never by truth.** This family exploits Keynes' own observation (Chapter 5) that long-term expectations receive no feedback. In *self-confirming equilibrium* (Fudenberg–Levine 1993), beliefs must match observed play on the equilibrium path, but off-path beliefs are unconstrained — and long-term expectation is largely an off-path object: a view about contingencies not being sampled. A convention is then a shared belief that is self-confirming without being correct, stable until events force the untested region onto the path; crisis is the day the off-path region gets sampled. In *Berk–Nash equilibrium* (Esponda–Pouzo 2016), agents hold misspecified models and converge to the best-fitting wrong belief: "project the present forward" is such a model, and its stability-then-collapse dynamics are the object of study rather than an embarrassment. *Eductive stability* (Guesnerie 1992) asks whether agents could reason their way to rational expectations by mental iteration; where eductive stability fails — commonly, under strategic complementarity, i.e., in Keynesian settings — the mathematics itself certifies that calculation cannot deliver coordination, and expectation must be anchored by something else. *Heterogeneous predictor ecologies* (Brock–Hommes 1997, 1998) supply the best existing dynamics of convention adoption: agents choose among forecasting rules by recent performance, conventions are the rules, and endogenous switching generates bubbles and crashes — though the rule set itself is exogenous.

**Family 3: bounded-depth reasoning anchored by salience.** Level-$k$ and cognitive-hierarchy models (Nagel 1995; Camerer–Ho–Chong 2004) have agents reason finitely many steps about others, anchored on a "level-0" specification. Level-0 is usually treated as a nuisance parameter; for this program it is the treasure: **level-0 is the formal slot where convention resides.** The reasoning is forward-looking about others, non-equilibrium, and the anchor is not derived but socially given — which is what Chapter 12 says. Lanteri and Carabelli's complaint (Section 6.1) that the experimental literature mis-translated Keynes becomes constructive here: put a Schelling-focal level-0, rather than a uniform-random one, into a macro model. Weinstein–Yildiz (2007) belongs to this family as its license: their structure theorem shows that with rich enough higher-order uncertainty, game-theoretic predictions are hostage to infinite-order beliefs no observer could check — a proof, from inside the Bayesian citadel, that the calculable-beliefs prong of the fork is unavailable. It is the mathematics of *why* calculation gives out.

**Family 4: the conceptual situation itself — awareness, cases, frames.** *Unawareness structures* (Modica–Rustichini 1999; Heifetz–Meier–Schipper 2006) model agents who cannot conceive of some contingencies at all — a reading of "we simply do not know" stronger than ambiguity, and the only one that supplies a new mapping for the paper: the "violent revision" of convention as an **awareness shock** — 2008 as the moment agents became aware of states absent from their model, an event no probability-based formalism can represent. *Case-based decision theory* (Gilboa–Schmeidler 1995) replaces state spaces and priors with decisions by similarity to remembered cases: "assume the existing state of affairs will continue" is a similarity judgment, not a probability, making CBDT arguably the most direct mathematics of the Chapter 12 heuristic; a shared case library plus a shared similarity function *is* a convention, connecting downward to the Lewis–Sugden precedent literature. *Variable-frame theory* (Bacharach) treats salience as the description under which options are conceived — the only formalism in the survey that makes Schelling focality a primitive rather than reducing it to information (B.1's failure) or invoking it by fiat (B.2's).

**The criterion the survey yields.** Every candidate retains a calculation somewhere; that is what mathematics is. What distinguishes the promising ones is that they stop trying to *derive* the conventional element and instead give it an explicit, exogenous, inspectable slot: the prior set (Family 1), the misspecified model or predictor set (Family 2), the level-0 anchor (Family 3), the awareness horizon or similarity function (Family 4). This suggests the success criterion for the program: **a formalization hosts conventional judgment not by explaining it away but by localizing it** — making the model's dependence on the socially given part explicit and, ideally, measurable. This criterion also resolves the Favereau worry of Section 8 (formalization as domestication): domestication is what happens when the conventional element is derived out of existence; localization is the alternative. And it forces one correction to B.2's own self-assessment: B.2 condemned its σ-jump technology $\mu(\sigma)$ as "Schelling-focality by fiat." Under the localization criterion the sin is not exogeneity — every candidate here has an exogenous slot — but *unmeasurability*. An exogenous $\mu(\sigma)$ disciplined by data (survey-measured willingness to follow the announcer, of the kind Appendix A's trust instruments approximate) is a localization; an exogenous $\mu(\sigma)$ free to take whatever value the modeler needs is a fudge. The line between hosting a convention and assuming one's conclusion runs through measurement, not through model structure.

**A composite architecture, and its risk.** On this criterion, the most promising single construction is a hybrid: an Epstein–Wang-style indeterminate equilibrium (so the economy formally *needs* a convention — ambiguity supplies the vacancy), closed by a level-0 salience anchor or a Berk–Nash shared model (so the convention is forward-looking and collapsible — the anchor fills the vacancy), with ν entering as the stickiness of the anchor (the norm defends it). It does not escape notice that this reproduces the σ–ν–λ architecture one level down. That reading cuts both ways and should be recorded honestly: read favorably, it is evidence of the framework's coherence — the informal triad reappears when the formal problem is set up independently; read unfavorably, it is a warning that the framework may be reproducing itself in its own image, organizing whatever material it is given. The discriminating test is the one this appendix has used throughout: build the hybrid and look for the places it *refuses* to deliver the verbal model's claims. If it refuses nothing, the unfavorable reading wins.
