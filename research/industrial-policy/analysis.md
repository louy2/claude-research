# China's industrial parks and poverty reduction — what worked, what didn't, and how the structural setup compares to Southeast Asia

This is a working synthesis, not a literature review. It threads three
anchor sources together — Keynes (the macro frame), Smith (the recent op-ed
synthesis), and Fernandes & Reed (the new World Bank empirical menu) —
plus four empirical papers that the World Bank report and Smith both
lean on (Lu/Wang/Zhu 2019 on Chinese SEZs; Lay/Tafese/Tran 2025 on
Vietnamese SEZs; Abagna/Hornok/Mulyukova 2025 on African SEZs;
Evenett et al. 2025 IMF WP on the post-2009 industrial-policy turn).

## 1. Frame

Keynes gives us the macro logic of why parks should help poverty at all:
poverty in low-income economies is mostly *underemployment* of rural labour
at very low marginal product. The binding constraint is not productivity
potential but **effective demand for labour at a wage above subsistence**.
A park is a coordinated bundle — land + power + customs + tenants — that
manufactures effective demand for labour in a specific location.

The micro-mechanism is in *General Theory* Chapter 12 §VIII, not §V's
animal-spirits passage that gets all the press: Keynes argues that
public utilities, monopoly-protected long-occupier-contract assets, and
**state-directed projects** sidestep the deep-uncertainty problem that
suppresses ordinary private investment. An industrial park is exactly
this kind of object. The park converts a high-uncertainty private
investment ("will Vietnamese suppliers exist? will Vietnamese power
hold? will customs work?") into a near-certain near-term yield with a
guaranteed buyer for inputs. That conversion is the policy work parks
are doing — and it is why they pay where standalone subsidies do not.

The corresponding Chapter 24 §III passage that the World Bank report
implicitly relies on:

> "It is not the ownership of the instruments of production which it is
> important for the State to assume. If the State is able to determine
> the aggregate amount of resources devoted to augmenting the
> instruments and the basic rate of reward to those who own them, it
> will have accomplished all that is necessary."

That is the cleanest theoretical defence of the FDI-park model: the
state coordinates and finances investment without owning the firms.

Fernandes & Reed take the same intuition and operationalise it as a
preference hierarchy — the **"Four I's"**: Institutions > Public
Inputs > Microeconomic Incentives > Macroeconomic Interventions. Parks
are the flagship Public Input. Subsidies and tariffs are
microeconomic incentives, treated as last-resort tools.

The report's empirical headline matters here: in the briefing's words,
upper-middle-income countries now run business subsidies averaging
**4.2% of GDP** — the highest on record — and the IMF's NIPO data
(Evenett et al. 2025) shows the post-2020 global wave is mostly subsidies
plus trade restrictions in advanced-tech sectors. So the "Tier 3"
instruments are precisely what most governments are scaling up, against
the report's advice. Smith's diagnosis of late-China — overlapping
subsidies, excess capacity, deflation, balance-sheet damage — is one
data point inside that broader pattern, not a stand-alone Chinese
pathology.

That is the lens. Now apply it.

## 2. China industrial parks as a poverty-reduction device

### What worked

1. **Labour reallocation at unprecedented scale.** Coastal SEZs
   (Shenzhen, Zhuhai, Xiamen, Shantou, then Pudong, Suzhou, Tianjin
   Binhai, Chongqing, etc.) absorbed hundreds of millions of rural
   migrants into wage manufacturing between roughly 1985 and 2015. This
   is the single largest Lewis-style transition in history; mechanically,
   it is also the largest poverty-reduction event in history. The park
   was the conveyor belt.

2. **A measurable, on-top wage premium for SEZ treatment.** Lu, Wang &
   Zhu (2019, AEJ Econ. Policy) identify the policy-attributable effect
   inside the broader migration story: zone designation raises local
   employment, capital, output, and productivity, with a wage premium of
   roughly **8% above the control group against ~5% higher local cost of
   living** — a real wage gain on the order of 3 pp net of inflation.
   Most of the effect comes from **net entry of new firms**, not
   incumbent expansion, which is the Fernandes–Reed coordination-
   failure mechanism observed in the field.

3. **Remittances back to the village.** Because hukou kept migrants
   formally rural, earnings flowed back to rural households as
   remittances rather than getting absorbed into urban consumption. This
   had the perverse-but-real effect of compressing the rural–urban
   income gap on the rural side without requiring an explicit rural
   transfer programme. The park indirectly funded the village.

4. **Infrastructure complementarity.** Chinese parks were unusually
   well-bundled with deep-water ports, dedicated rail, reliable power,
   and bonded customs — the World Bank briefing's exact "principles of
   practice" for industrial-park siting (slide 14: locate near labour
   pools and ports/transport hubs; bundle with utilities and schooling).
   This is the Public-Input toolkit done at industrial scale.

5. **Cadre incentives aligned with park performance.** Local Party
   secretaries were promoted on GDP, FDI, and tax growth, all of which
   tracked park occupancy. So the political principal-agent problem
   that plagues SEZs elsewhere (officials pocket rents, parks remain
   ghost developments) was muted, at least on the coast and in the
   first decade. This is the Chinese version of the "MITI archetype" —
   technocratic insulation, performance-rewarded — that the World Bank
   briefing flags as the institutional precondition for parks to work
   (slides 29–30).

6. **Diaspora capital seeded the early waves.** Hong Kong, Taiwanese,
   and overseas-Chinese FDI dominated the Pearl River Delta launch.
   This is a real one-off advantage that most SEA countries don't have
   to the same degree (Vietnam partly does, via PRC investment;
   Thailand via Japanese; Indonesia much less).

7. **Backward linkages eventually formed.** Unlike pure assembly
   enclaves, coastal Chinese clusters developed deep domestic supplier
   ecosystems (Dongguan electronics, Yiwu small commodities, Suzhou
   precision components). The poverty channel kept widening as second-
   and third-tier suppliers spun up.

### What didn't work, or worked badly

1. **Land expropriation taxed the rural base.** Park land was assembled
   cheaply by local governments through compulsory acquisition at
   agricultural-use prices, then leased to industrial users at much
   higher prices. The fiscal surplus financed the park, but the
   implicit tax fell on dispossessed farmers. The park reduced poverty
   *in expectation* by drawing migrants in, while creating poverty *in
   fact* for those who lost land and didn't get jobs.

2. **Hukou exclusion produced a second-class urban underclass.**
   Migrant workers in the parks faced higher tuition, worse health
   access, and no pension portability. Their children ("left-behind
   children" if kept in the village, or "floating children" if brought
   along) bore a real welfare cost that doesn't show up in headline
   poverty statistics.

3. **Inland "ghost parks."** The post-2000 Western Development drive
   replicated the coastal park template across inland provinces with
   much weaker preconditions (no port, thinner labour markets, weaker
   cadre incentives). A large share of these zones run at low occupancy
   and serve mostly as a vehicle for local-government land finance
   rather than for employment. In Keynesian terms this is exactly the
   §V "casino" failure mode applied to LGFV-financed land speculation:
   the convention that "tenants will arrive because they did in the
   coastal case" was the prevailing investment heuristic, and it broke.

4. **The post-2010 pivot to subsidies has the unprofitability problem.**
   When the state moved from "build a park, attract foreigners" to
   "subsidize a hundred domestic champions in EVs / solar / batteries /
   semiconductors", it landed in the World Bank report's last-resort
   tier. Smith's 2026-update diagnosis — overlapping subsidies, excess
   capacity, deflation, balance-sheet damage, and now a possible
   bad-debt wave when subsidy recipients are forced into bankruptcy —
   matches Fernandes & Reed's warning that subsidies need *automatic
   termination* with a cost-per-unit downward trend to extend, not
   open-ended renewal. Late China violates that discipline.

5. **Local-government debt buildup.** Park-financing through LGFVs
   created a contingent fiscal liability that is now a macro overhang.
   This is the downside of the "self-financing park" model when land
   values stop rising. The Fernandes–Reed line that parks "may pay
   upfront but recoup quickly by selling units to occupants" depends on
   tenancy actually materialising; for a meaningful share of inland
   zones it didn't.

6. **Capital-intensity skew at the late-coastal frontier.** Lu, Wang &
   Zhu show that *even within the successful coastal sample*, capital-
   intensive industries gained more than labour-intensive ones from
   zone designation. This is consistent with the post-2010 attenuation
   of the poverty channel: as Chinese SEZs upgraded technologically,
   the marginal job created moved up the skill ladder, away from the
   rural surplus-labour pool that the early-coastal phase absorbed.

7. **Environmental and occupational-health externalities.** Lightly
   priced into the early model; now showing up as health costs that
   fall disproportionately on migrant workers — i.e., on the same group
   that benefited most from the wage channel. Net welfare gain is
   still positive but smaller than the headline.

### Net read

The early-coastal-park phase (1985 to ~2008) is one of the cleanest
empirical successes in the development literature, and it sits exactly
at the intersection of (a) the Keynesian state-directed-project logic
in *GT* Ch. 12 §VIII, (b) the "socialisation of investment without
ownership" of Ch. 24 §III, and (c) the Fernandes–Reed Public-Inputs
tier. The post-2010 phase has drifted into the Microeconomic-Incentives
tier — subsidies, champions, capacity races — and is running into the
predicted failure modes documented for that tier worldwide (Evenett et
al. 2025). Treating "China's industrial policy" as a single monolithic
story conflates these two phases and is the most common mistake in the
popular discourse.

## 3. Structural pros and cons vs Southeast Asia

By "SEA" here I mean primarily Vietnam, Thailand, Malaysia, Indonesia,
and the Philippines, with Cambodia and Bangladesh as low-end appendices.
The Lay/Tafese/Tran (2025) Vietnam study is the closest Southeast Asian
analogue to Lu/Wang/Zhu and the strongest comparator for the discussion.

### China's structural advantages

- **Continental scale.** Even a single park sits inside a 1.4-billion-
  person domestic market and labour pool. Agglomeration economies
  inside the park reach a self-reinforcing threshold faster than in any
  single SEA country. A Vietnamese zone of the same physical size has
  a thinner local supplier base to draw on.
- **State capacity for land assembly and infrastructure.** The Chinese
  state can build a deep-water port, a six-lane highway, and a rail
  spur on the timeline a foreign tenant requires. Indonesia and the
  Philippines cannot, and this shows up in repeatedly delayed zone
  launches. Vietnam is closer to China on this dimension; Thailand is
  in the middle.
- **Single-tier policy implementation.** The Party can override local
  holdouts, NIMBY litigation, and competing line ministries. SEA
  democracies pay a real coordination tax here. The cost is the
  welfare side (hukou, expropriation); the benefit is speed.
- **Ecosystem density.** Twenty years of coastal supplier formation
  means a new tenant in Suzhou or Dongguan finds ten qualified
  component vendors within a day's drive. Vietnam is approaching this
  for textiles and smartphones; Malaysia has it for E&E packaging;
  nobody else in SEA has it yet.

### China's structural disadvantages

- **Wages have already converged out of the low-skill labour-intensive
  band.** Cambodia, Bangladesh, Vietnam, and increasingly Indonesia
  inherit garments, footwear, and basic assembly. The poverty-reduction
  channel that made early Chinese parks magic is now an SEA channel.
  Lay/Tafese/Tran 2025 documents the Vietnamese version of exactly the
  shift that Lu/Wang/Zhu documented for early-coastal China — same
  research design, same direction of effect, with the additional
  female-concentrated distributional gain that Chinese SEZs did not
  deliver in the same way.
- **Demographic reversal.** China's working-age population is
  shrinking; Vietnam, Indonesia, and the Philippines still have a
  demographic dividend. The marginal worker entering a Chinese park is
  no longer a destitute 18-year-old farmer; it is increasingly an
  internal transfer from one urban job to another, which yields much
  less poverty reduction per FDI dollar.
- **Geopolitical risk discount.** US export controls, tariffs, and
  inbound-FDI screening now penalise multinationals for adding capacity
  in China. The Evenett et al. 2025 IMF data shows this is part of a
  global structural-break-around-2020 turn toward national-security and
  resilience-motivated policy. SEA — especially Vietnam, Malaysia,
  Thailand, and India outside ASEAN — collects the "China + 1"
  reshoring premium. The same FDI flow that built Shenzhen in 1990
  partly builds Bac Ninh and Penang in 2026.
- **Welfare friction (hukou).** SEA migrants keep their citizenship and
  political voice; Chinese migrants don't, fully. This makes SEA's
  poverty-reduction gains more durable in welfare-state terms even
  when the headline growth rate is lower.
- **Less rule-of-law signal for high-end FDI.** R&D centers and
  regional HQs increasingly favour Singapore, Penang, Kuala Lumpur,
  Bangkok over inland Chinese cities for non-economic reasons (data
  governance, arbitration, language, expat life).

### SEA's structural advantages

- **ASEAN + RCEP + CPTPP market access.** A Vietnamese park exports
  duty-free into a much larger network of agreements than a Chinese
  park currently does. This compensates for the smaller domestic base.
  In the Fernandes–Reed framing, this *partly relaxes the "local
  market size" feasibility constraint* that would otherwise limit
  SEA's industrial-policy options.
- **Multiplicity of competing zone authorities.** Vietnam, Malaysia,
  Indonesia, and Thailand each run several zone regimes that compete
  on terms. This produces some experimentation that a unitary Chinese
  system doesn't.
- **English-language and common-law overlays in parts of the region.**
  Singapore, Penang, and Manila are easier landing pads for global
  professional services and HQ functions than any Chinese coastal city
  except Hong Kong.
- **Distributional gains skew toward women.** The Vietnamese SEZ
  evidence shows female workers and rural districts gain
  disproportionately. The Chinese SEZ literature does not have an
  equally clean version of this finding, partly because Chinese SEZ
  employment was less female-concentrated and partly because the
  relevant household-welfare data were never as tractable. SEA's
  poverty-reduction story therefore has a stronger gender-equity
  channel than China's did.

### SEA's structural disadvantages

- **State capacity is uneven.** Indonesia in particular suffers from
  fragmented land tenure, court delays, and weak local-government
  coordination. The Philippines under-invests in transport. Park
  performance is bimodal: a Penang or a Bac Ninh works; a backwater
  zone in Sulawesi or Mindanao does not.
- **Smaller domestic markets.** Backward linkages form more slowly.
  The Malaysian E&E sector is the textbook cautionary tale: forty
  years of FDI parks, world-class assembly, and yet a thin layer of
  indigenous design capability — the middle-income trap as failed
  deep-linkage policy.
- **Demographic dividend won't last forever.** Thailand is already
  aging. Vietnam will follow within ~15 years. The window in which SEA
  can run the early-Chinese playbook is finite, which raises the
  urgency of doing the Fernandes–Reed Public-Input tier (skills, R&D,
  competition policy) properly *now* rather than later.
- **Termination-discipline risk.** Indonesia's history of protecting
  national champions (cars, aircraft, banks) is a cautionary tale of
  exactly the failure mode Fernandes & Reed flag with their Argentina
  Tierra-del-Fuego case study: an electronics special regime
  established in 1972 with a 35-year sunset has been extended to
  2038, a 66-year horizon, while still failing to be internationally
  competitive. SEA democracies face a similar political-economy
  dynamic if they slide into Microeconomic-Incentives-tier
  instruments without the discipline.

## 4. Implications for how to think about the question

1. **The right comparison is not "China vs SEA" but "early China vs SEA
   today."** They are running approximately the same playbook. The
   Vietnamese (Lay/Tafese/Tran 2025) and African (Abagna/Hornok/Mulyukova
   2025) SEZ papers now document with comparable identification
   strategies the same kinds of effects Lu/Wang/Zhu (2019) documented
   for early Chinese coastal zones. The relevant question is whether
   SEA can avoid the *late*-China failure modes (subsidy escalation,
   ghost parks, welfare-rights exclusion), not whether it can replicate
   Shenzhen.

2. **Industrial parks reduce poverty through the labour-reallocation
   channel — and the local-spillover channel.** Lay/Tafese/Tran's
   finding that earnings improvements spill over from foreign-firm
   workers to household-business and domestic-firm workers is the
   strongest piece of evidence that the SEZ effect is not just an
   inside-the-fence transfer. Together with Abagna/Hornok/Mulyukova's
   ~10-km wealth-effect radius for African SEZs, the cross-continental
   evidence base for "parks raise local welfare" is now considerably
   stronger than it was even five years ago.

3. **The Fernandes–Reed scepticism on subsidies and tariffs is best
   read as a discipline argument, not an ideological one.** Subsidies
   can work (Korea HCI, Japan MITI, early-China for some sectors), but
   only when the institutional discipline — sunset clauses, export-
   performance conditionality, exit enforcement — holds. The 12-lesson
   menu's principle is concrete: *3-year evaluation milestones, up to
   10-year persistence, extension only with declining cost-per-unit
   evidence vs non-targeted industries*. The Argentine and Indonesian
   counter-examples are what you get when those rules are absent.

4. **What the Keynesian frame adds is that the state's job is
   *coordination*, not selection or ownership.** The cleanest reading
   of *GT* Ch. 12 §VIII plus Ch. 24 §III is: bundle the long-term
   commitments that no single private actor will make first (port, grid,
   customs, anchor tenant), then let private actors — including foreign
   ones — choose what to build. A park is the coordination device; the
   firms inside it should be chosen by markets, including foreign
   markets. The closer Chinese policy stays to that logic, the better
   it has worked. The further it has drifted into picking domestic
   champions, the worse.

5. **Hukou is a genuine, structural negative externality of the Chinese
   model that doesn't have an SEA analogue.** A poverty-reduction
   accounting that captures this would narrow the China-vs-SEA welfare
   gap substantially even though it would not flip the sign.

6. **The global subsidy turn is now the relevant headwind for
   *both* China and SEA.** Evenett et al. 2025's NIPO data show a
   worldwide shift toward subsidies and trade restrictions in
   advanced-tech sectors after 2020. SEA's "China + 1" tailwind has to
   compete with this rich-country reshoring of the same sectors back
   home. The space for the FDI-park playbook is bigger than it was in
   2010 (because of geopolitical diversification) but more contested
   than it was (because rich countries are bidding for the same
   factories with their own subsidy budgets).

## 5. Open threads worth pulling on next

- **Numerical comparison Chinese SEZs vs Vietnamese SEZs.** Lu/Wang/Zhu
  give an ~8% wage premium net of cost-of-living for early Chinese
  zones. The corresponding Vietnamese magnitudes are paywalled in the
  Lay/Tafese/Tran paper but should be retrievable in a follow-up; in
  the same units these would put the "early-China-like" claim onto
  comparable footing.
- **The "ghost park" base rate.** What share of Chinese parks fail vs
  SEA parks vs African ones? Abagna/Hornok/Mulyukova show that
  *successful* African SEZs do raise local wealth meaningfully; the
  selection question — what fraction become successful — is the
  remaining unknown.
- **Specific termination-discipline rules SEA could legislate.** The
  Fernandes–Reed 12-lesson menu is concrete (3-year milestone, 10-year
  cap, declining-cost-per-unit extension test). A short comparative
  inventory of which SEA countries have any of these in writing would
  be useful.
- **The Keynesian re-framing.** Is the FDI-park model best described as
  *socialisation of coordination* rather than *socialisation of
  investment*? The Ch. 12 §VIII plus Ch. 24 §III combination supports
  this read more cleanly than the Ch. 24 §III passage on its own. That
  would be a small but useful theoretical contribution and a good
  short follow-up note.
- **The 4.2%-of-GDP subsidy frontier.** The Fernandes–Reed datapoint —
  upper-middle-income subsidies averaging 4.2% of GDP — is novel and
  underscored. Worth checking what fraction of that is Chinese, and
  what the SEA subscores look like, to test the late-China-as-
  representative claim quantitatively.
