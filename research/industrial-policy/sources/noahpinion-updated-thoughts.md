## Thesis

Industrial policy is back on the global research agenda, and the empirical
verdict is finally getting sharper. Two recent reviews — the IMF's
*Industrial Policy Since the Great Financial Crisis* (Evenett et al.
2025, IMF WP 2025/222) and the new World Bank policy research report by
Fernandes and Reed — both converge on the same pattern:

- **Export promotion + FDI attraction + industrial parks**: works.
- **Import substitution + broad subsidies + tariffs**: usually doesn't.

Smith treats this as vindication of what he has been calling the
"Poland/Malaysia model" (and elsewhere the Singapore and Ireland model):
get rich by hosting other people's multinationals.

## On Poland and Malaysia

Smith singled out Poland and Malaysia as countries that "got rich in
recent years simply by encouraging multinational companies to put their
factories and research centers there." He notes that Poland "is about to
surpass Japan's living standards, and is now even starting to catch up to
Korea" — even though it does not have internationally recognised domestic
brands. Implication: the FDI-park model is a development strategy, not a
brand-building strategy.

## On China

- Pre-2010: China grew by absorbing FDI in coastal zones, much like
  Poland/Malaysia. This is the phase Smith is bullish about and that maps
  onto the Fernandes–Reed parks-as-public-input framing.
- Post-2010: China pivoted to massive subsidies for national champions
  (EVs, solar, batteries, semiconductors). Smith argues this newer phase
  has an "unprofitability problem":
  - Direct quote: *"paying dozens of companies to all make the same
    products ends up creating brutal price wars that compete profit
    margins toward zero."*
  - Direct quote: *"if and when China forces most of its subsidy
    recipients into bankruptcy … it'll result in a huge wave of bad
    debts."*
  - The financial-instability framing is the new piece in the 2026
    update — Smith is now explicitly worried about a Japan-style
    bad-debt overhang from late-stage Chinese subsidy policy.

## Three refinements Smith makes to his earlier views

1. **Definitional discipline.** "Industrial policy" is too broad a term
   to argue about productively. Both the IMF and World Bank papers
   distinguish effective tools (parks, market-access, FDI promotion) from
   ineffective ones (broad subsidies, defensive tariffs, import
   substitution). The bundle term obscures the empirical signal.
2. **FDI-park as a "general-purpose" recipe.** It is replicable, doesn't
   require picking winners, doesn't need a Hamiltonian state, and layers
   on top of "boring" basics (macro stability, schooling, ports,
   electricity). This is the closest thing to a transferable development
   playbook.
3. **Tech policy in rich countries is industrial policy.** US support for
   internet infrastructure and current AI initiatives are forms of
   "picking winners" in genuinely new technologies — a practice that
   even free-market sceptics broadly accept when it's framed as
   tech-promotion rather than industrial policy.

## Analytical takeaway Smith pushes

Industrial parks plus FDI promotion may be the closest thing to a
*general-purpose* development recipe.

Subsidies and tariffs are not useless but the bar is much higher: they
only pay off in narrow, well-disciplined, time-bound cases, and most
political systems can't sustain that discipline. Late-stage China is
Smith's central case in point.

## How this article frames the user's question

Smith's piece sets up a sharp contrast that is exactly the one the user
wants to interrogate: the **FDI-park model** (which works in Poland,
Malaysia, and *early* China) vs. the **subsidy/champion model** (which
has problems even in *late* China). For the China-vs-SEA comparison,
Smith effectively argues that SEA is doing the thing that worked in
early China, while late China is doing something newer and shakier. The
2026 update adds the financial-system tail risk: late-China subsidy
deflation could end in a banking crisis the textbook poverty story
doesn't capture.
