## Thesis

Industrial policy is back on the global research agenda, and the empirical
verdict is finally getting sharper. Two recent reviews — one from the IMF
and a new World Bank policy research report by Fernandes and Reed — both
converge on the same pattern:

- **Export promotion + FDI attraction + industrial parks**: works.
- **Import substitution + broad subsidies + tariffs**: usually doesn't.

Smith treats this as vindication of what he has been calling the
"Poland/Malaysia model" (and elsewhere the Singapore and Ireland model):
get rich by hosting other people's multinationals.

## Key claims (paraphrased / quoted from search snippets)

- "The new World Bank report focuses on industrial parks as its favorite
  industrial policy. Industrial parks are a key part of the
  Poland/Malaysia/Singapore/Ireland strategy — a tool of FDI promotion."
- "The IMF paper explicitly contrasts export promotion with import
  substitution (protectionism), claiming that the former is very promising
  while the latter is usually bad. The World Bank report supports industrial
  parks and market-access assistance, while casting doubt on the
  effectiveness of subsidies and tariffs."
- "Poland and Malaysia have succeeded at getting rich in recent years simply
  by encouraging multinational companies to put their factories and research
  centers there."
- On China: pre-2010 China grew by absorbing FDI in coastal zones, much like
  Poland/Malaysia. After ~2010 it pivoted to massive subsidies for national
  champions; Smith argues this newer phase has an "unprofitability problem"
  — overlapping subsidies create excess capacity, price wars, deflation, and
  bad debt, even when output and exports look impressive.

## Analytical takeaway Smith pushes

Industrial parks plus FDI promotion may be the closest thing to a *general-
purpose* development recipe — replicable, doesn't require picking winners,
doesn't need a Hamiltonian state, layers cleanly on top of "boring" basics
(macro stability, schooling, ports, electricity).

Subsidies and tariffs are not useless but the bar is much higher: they only
pay off in narrow, well-disciplined, time-bound cases, and most political
systems can't sustain that discipline.

## How this article frames the user's question

Smith's piece sets up a sharp contrast that is exactly the one the user
wants to interrogate: the **FDI-park model** (which works in Poland, Malaysia,
and *early* China) vs. the **subsidy/champion model** (which has problems
even in *late* China). For the China-vs-SEA comparison, Smith effectively
argues that SEA is doing the thing that worked in early China, while late
China is doing something newer and shakier.
