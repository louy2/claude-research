# Abagna, Hornok & Mulyukova (2025), "Place-based Policies and Household Wealth in Africa"

## Why it matters here

This is the load-bearing empirical citation underneath the World Bank
report's claim that industrial parks raise household welfare. Slide 14
of the technical briefing cites it directly for the African evidence.
Having the underlying paper, not just the briefing line, lets us judge
the strength of the SEZ-as-poverty-reduction claim outside the East
Asian context.

## Methodology

- Repeated cross-sections of households living at varying distances
  around SEZs in **10 African countries**, **1990–2020** (the specific
  10 countries are listed in the paper's data appendix; the paper
  webpages don't reproduce them).
- Identifies effects from variation across distance bands and timing of
  SEZ establishment.
- Uses Demographic and Health Survey-style household wealth indices,
  not consumption or income directly.

## Key findings

1. Households near SEZs become **significantly wealthier** vs the
   national average after the zone is established.
2. Effects are **most pronounced within ~10 km** of the zone and
   **decline rapidly with distance** — a tight spatial gradient that
   makes a causal-place-based-policy interpretation more credible.
3. Effects are **strongest at the middle of the wealth distribution** —
   not at the very top or bottom. This matters for poverty-reduction
   claims: SEZs lift the median household more than they lift the
   poorest, which is consistent with skill thresholds for park
   employment.
4. Channels: increased access to household utilities, higher consumption
   of durable goods, higher educational attainment, and a shift away
   from agricultural employment.
5. The effect is **not driven by residential sorting** of wealthier
   households into SEZ neighbourhoods — addresses the obvious
   selection-bias objection.

## Caveats

- Wealth-index outcomes (durables ownership, utilities access) are
  better measured in DHS data than wages or consumption are; some of
  the apparent gain may be intra-household reallocation toward goods the
  index captures.
- 10-km radius is wide; some "nearby" households may be in unrelated
  urban catchments.
- The 10-country set is probably skewed toward African countries where
  SEZs were more developed by 2010 (Ethiopia, Morocco, Egypt, Senegal,
  Nigeria are likely candidates), so external validity to e.g.
  landlocked low-state-capacity contexts is limited.

## Implication for the analysis

The Africa SEZ literature has historically been the strongest
counter-example to "parks always work" — most academic surveys (e.g.,
Farole 2011) catalogued African zones with low occupancy and weak
spillovers. This 2025 paper updates that picture: when an African SEZ is
*built and tenanted*, the local welfare effect is real and large enough
to detect in DHS data. The remaining variance is between zones, not
between continents.

This sharpens the analysis claim that "industrial parks work as a class
but not in every case" — the cross-continental evidence now
substantially supports the claim. It also makes "ghost park" failure a
question of selection and execution, not of geography per se.
