## Why it's a useful anchor for industrial-policy thinking

Keynes is not an industrial-policy theorist in the modern sense, but the
*General Theory* supplies the macro frame that pro-industrial-park
arguments implicitly rely on:

1. **Effective demand (Books I–III).** Output and employment are
   determined by aggregate demand, not by Say's-Law supply identities.
   Voluntary unemployment is not the binding constraint; underutilization
   of labor and capital is. For developing economies with surplus rural
   labor, the binding constraint is creating wage-paying demand for that
   labor.

2. **The marginal efficiency of capital and the role of expectations
   (Chapter 11–12).** Private investment is volatile because it depends
   on long-run expectations under fundamental uncertainty. This is the
   theoretical opening for coordinated public action.

3. **The socialisation of investment (Chapter 24, "Concluding Notes on
   the Social Philosophy towards which the General Theory might Lead").**

4. **Liquidity preference (Chapter 13–17).** The interest rate is partly a
   monetary phenomenon, set by policy — undergirds the development-finance
   side of industrial parks.

## Chapter 12: long-term expectation and animal spirits

Section structure:

- **§I–II** Long-term expectations rest on a thin basis of knowledge plus
  an anchoring "convention": *"Our usual practice being to take the
  existing situation and to project it into the future."* Confidence
  matters as much as central forecasts.
- **§III** Knowledge of yields ten years out *"amounts to little and
  sometimes to nothing."* Before organised stock markets, illiquidity
  forced commitment; modern markets removed that discipline.
- **§IV** The "convention" assumption that current valuations are
  approximately correct is what makes individual investments feel liquid
  even though the social capital stock is fixed. The convention is
  fragile.
- **§V** Five precariousness factors: (1) ownership separated from
  knowledge; (2) ephemeral noise driving valuations; (3) herd
  psychology; (4) professional speculation dominating long-term
  enterprise — the famous *beauty-contest analogy*: *"We devote our
  intelligences to anticipating what average opinion expects the average
  opinion to be"*; (5) credit-confidence collapse.
  The casino metaphor: *"When the capital development of a country
  becomes a by-product of the activities of a casino"* the result is
  bad.
- **§VI** Speculation vs enterprise. Speculation can ride on top of
  enterprise harmlessly *as bubbles on a steady stream of enterprise*,
  but the inverse is dangerous. Heavier transfer taxes (cf. London
  jobber fees) might discipline churn but face a liquidity dilemma.
- **§VII** **Animal spirits.** *"Most, probably, of our decisions to do
  something positive … can only be taken as a result of animal spirits —
  of a spontaneous urge to action rather than inaction."* Enterprise
  cannot rest on calculation alone because the probabilistic basis
  doesn't exist. Political atmospheres hostile to business can extinguish
  these spirits mechanically.
- **§VIII** Some investments — public utilities with monopoly
  guarantees, long-occupier-contract buildings, **state-directed
  projects** — sidestep this deep uncertainty. *"I am now somewhat
  sceptical of the success of a merely monetary policy directed towards
  influencing"* the rate of investment alone; the State must coordinate
  capital deployment directly.

**Why this matters for industrial-park policy.** A park is exactly the
kind of "state-directed project" §VIII discusses: it bundles long-term,
quasi-monopoly, infrastructural commitments that no single private
investor would underwrite first, but that, once present, lower the
animal-spirits hurdle for everyone else. The park converts a deep-
uncertainty private investment into a near-term yield with credible
demand for inputs. This is the Keynesian micro-mechanism for why parks
work where standalone subsidies don't.

The §V casino warning also cuts the other way for industrial policy:
*inland Chinese "ghost parks" are an animal-spirits failure as much as a
planning failure.* Local cadres betting on FDI tides that never arrived
were doing what §V identifies as the prevailing investment behaviour,
not departing from it.

## Chapter 24: concluding notes on social philosophy

Section structure:

- **§I — Wealth inequality and taxation.** The classical defence of
  inequality (the rich save, savings = investment) is wrong below full
  employment, where low consumption *suppresses* investment. Death
  duties and progressive taxation can boost consumption and investment
  simultaneously. Keynes accepts some inequality as psychologically
  useful: *"Much lower stakes will serve the purpose equally well, as
  soon as the players are accustomed to them."*
- **§II — Euthanasia of the rentier.** Falling interest rates will
  gradually eliminate rentier returns as capital becomes abundant: *"The
  euthanasia of the rentier, of the functionless investor, will be
  nothing sudden."* No revolution required; state-managed accumulation
  can sustain the process.
- **§III — Limited state intervention; defence of individualism.**
  Despite the central controls he proposes, Keynes wants most economic
  life to remain private. Classical analysis applies *once full
  employment is achieved*: *"Individualism … is the best safeguard of
  personal liberty."* The state's job is the *aggregate* of investment,
  not the firm-by-firm composition.

  Two famous passages from this section that bear directly on
  industrial-park design:

  > "I conceive, therefore, that a somewhat comprehensive socialisation
  > of investment will prove the only means of securing an approximation
  > to full employment; though this need not exclude all manner of
  > compromises and of devices by which public authority will co-operate
  > with private initiative."

  > "It is not the ownership of the instruments of production which it
  > is important for the State to assume. If the State is able to
  > determine the aggregate amount of resources devoted to augmenting
  > the instruments and the basic rate of reward to those who own them,
  > it will have accomplished all that is necessary."

- **§IV — Economic causes of war.** Competition for export markets under
  the gold standard is a structural cause of conflict. Domestic full
  employment removes that pressure: international trade *"would cease to
  be … a desperate expedient."* (Tangentially relevant to the
  geopolitical-risk discount on China-located FDI in §3 of the analysis.)
- **§V — The power of ideas over vested interests.** *"The world is
  ruled by little else"* than economists' and philosophers' ideas, even
  if it takes decades. Closing line: *"Practical men, who believe
  themselves to be quite exempt from any intellectual influences, are
  usually the slaves of some defunct economist."*

## What Keynes does *not* license

Two cautions worth carrying into the China/SEA discussion:

- Keynes's argument is for *aggregate* demand management and aggregate
  investment coordination, not for picking winners. Sectoral subsidies
  and tariffs are not derivable from the *General Theory* — they require
  additional industrial-organisation arguments (infant industry,
  externalities, learning-by-doing). The Fernandes–Reed scepticism on
  subsidies and tariffs is therefore consistent with Keynes, not against
  him.
- Chapter 12's animal-spirits warning cuts both ways: a zone built on
  speculation collapses on the same wave. Inland Chinese ghost parks are
  the empirical referent.
