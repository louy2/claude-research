## Why it's a useful anchor for industrial-policy thinking

Keynes is not an industrial-policy theorist in the modern sense, but the *General
Theory* supplies the macro frame that pro-industrial-park arguments implicitly
rely on:

1. **Effective demand (Books I–III).** Output and employment are determined by
   aggregate demand, not by Say's-Law supply identities. Voluntary unemployment
   is not the binding constraint; underutilization of labor and capital is.
   For developing economies with surplus rural labor, the binding constraint
   is creating wage-paying demand for that labor.

2. **The marginal efficiency of capital and the role of expectations
   (Chapter 11–12).** Private investment is volatile because it depends on
   long-run expectations under fundamental uncertainty. This is the
   theoretical opening for coordinated public action — in 1936 it justified
   counter-cyclical public works; in a 2026 development context it justifies
   the state assembling land, infrastructure, and tenants in the same place
   to make private investment commitments mutually credible. An industrial
   park is, in Keynesian terms, a coordination device for the marginal
   efficiency of capital.

3. **The socialisation of investment (Chapter 24, "Concluding Notes on the
   Social Philosophy towards which the General Theory might Lead").**
   Key passage:

   > "I conceive, therefore, that a somewhat comprehensive socialisation of
   > investment will prove the only means of securing an approximation to
   > full employment; though this need not exclude all manner of compromises
   > and of devices by which public authority will co-operate with private
   > initiative."

   And:

   > "It is not the ownership of the instruments of production which it is
   > important for the State to assume. If the State is able to determine the
   > aggregate amount of resources devoted to augmenting the instruments and
   > the basic rate of reward to those who own them, it will have accomplished
   > all that is necessary."

   This is the cleanest defence of a state that *coordinates and finances*
   investment without owning the firms — exactly the FDI-park model that the
   World Bank and Smith now favor. The state builds the park, sets the
   incentives, and lets foreign multinationals own the assets.

4. **Liquidity preference and interest rates (Chapter 13–17).** Keynes's
   point that the interest rate is a monetary phenomenon, set partly by
   policy, undergirds the development-finance side of industrial parks:
   subsidized credit, tax holidays, and bonded warehousing all lower the
   effective hurdle rate that private capital faces inside the zone.

## What Keynes does *not* license

Two cautions worth carrying into the China/SEA discussion:

- Keynes's argument is for *aggregate* demand management, not for picking
  winners. Sectoral subsidies and tariffs are not derivable from the *General
  Theory* — they require additional industrial-organization arguments
  (infant industry, externalities, learning-by-doing). The Fernandes–Reed
  scepticism on subsidies and tariffs is therefore consistent with Keynes,
  not against him.
- Chapter 12's warning about "animal spirits" cuts both ways: a zone built on
  a speculative wave of FDI will collapse on the same wave. Inland Chinese
  "ghost parks" are an animal-spirits failure as much as a planning failure.

## Most-cited passages for this topic

- "The outstanding faults of the economic society in which we live are its
  failure to provide for full employment and its arbitrary and inequitable
  distribution of wealth and incomes." (Ch. 24, opening)
- "Practical men, who believe themselves to be quite exempt from any
  intellectual influences, are usually the slaves of some defunct economist."
  (Ch. 24, closing)
