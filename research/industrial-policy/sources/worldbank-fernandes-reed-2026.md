## Scope

A menu of **15 industrial-policy tools**, drawing on new evidence from more
than **60 economies**, with guidance on (a) how to pick instruments to match
specific goals (jobs, exports, FX, pollution, security, resilience), (b) how
to design institutions, and (c) when each tool is more vs. less likely to
work.

Headline empirical finding the authors flag: **upper-middle-income countries
are the heaviest users of industrial policy**, with business subsidies
averaging ~4.2% of GDP — the highest on record — alongside higher and more
targeted tariffs than high-income countries use.

## Tool-level verdicts (as relayed in coverage)

Roughly tier-ranked by the report's enthusiasm:

- **Tier 1 — generally pro-growth, recommended:**
  - Industrial parks / SEZs (the report's *favorite* tool)
  - Market-access assistance (export promotion, trade facilitation)
  - FDI promotion bundled with the above
- **Tier 2 — works only with strong design:**
  - Skills programmes, R&D support, competition-preserving incentives,
    cluster policy
- **Tier 3 — sceptical:**
  - Broad business subsidies
  - Tariffs (especially defensive / import-substitution use)
  - Picking national champions

## Design principles emphasised

Industrial policy works best when it is:

1. **Disciplined** — clear KPIs, sunset clauses, enforced exit.
2. **Pro-competitive** — does not entrench incumbents.
3. **Time-bound** — automatic phase-out, not perpetual rents.
4. **Embedded in strong institutions** — rule of law, audit, transparency.
5. **Complemented** — only works on top of infrastructure, skills, and
   governance investments.

## Why industrial parks score so well in the report

- Locational sorting: park boundaries make incentives easy to monitor, so
  rents don't leak system-wide.
- Bundling: roads + power + customs + tax holiday addresses several binding
  constraints simultaneously, which a single subsidy line item cannot do.
- Self-financing path: successful parks throw off rates and land value that
  service the original infrastructure debt.
- Replicability: doesn't require sector-specific bets or sophisticated
  forecasting capacity, so even thin-bench bureaucracies can run them.

## Why subsidies/tariffs score so poorly

- Capture: difficult to cut once a constituency forms.
- Distortion: protect uncompetitive incumbents, which then under-invest in
  productivity.
- Coordination failure: when many countries subsidize the same sector, the
  price effect dominates the productivity effect (cf. Smith's "unprofitability"
  point about late-stage Chinese subsidies in EVs and solar).

## Caveats

- The report's evidence base is broader than China and includes mixed cases
  (e.g., African SEZs that under-performed). Industrial parks *as a class*
  outperform on average; that does not mean every park works, and a large
  share of zones in low-state-capacity contexts have failed.
- The report does not directly advance a poverty-reduction *causal* claim
  for SEZs — that channel has to be argued via labor-reallocation and
  wage-premium evidence in country case studies.
