## Headline framing (from briefing slides 1–9)

The report's working definition: **"Industrial policy is a government
action expected to increase a strategic business activity."**

- "Strategic" = government has decided one activity matters more than others.
- "Industrial" is *not* just manufacturing — it covers agribusiness, mining,
  professional services, tourism.
- A March 2025 survey of 32 World Bank Prosperity lead economists found
  **80% had been asked by clients for industrial-policy advice in the prior
  year**.
- Motivations in developing-country clients are "mostly jobs and
  investment" — explicitly *not* the supply-chain-resilience and security
  motivations that dominate IMF's NIPO dataset for advanced economies
  (Evenett et al. 2024/5).

The report claims that for the *development* goal — as opposed to
resilience or security — industrial policy *in theory involves no
trade-offs*: any business activity with positive spillovers (i.e., a
market failure) is strategic, and budget-neutral subsidies for such
activities grow GDP. (Slide 5.)

It then immediately notes the empirical caveats: most evidence is for
manufacturing; most evidence is on "effectiveness" not "efficiency"
(benefit > cost is rarely tested directly).

## The three feasibility constraints

Slides 7–9 collapse "what tools can a country actually use" into three
country characteristics:

1. **Government bandwidth** — workforce and institutional set-up for
   public-private interaction. Constrains tools that require deep firm-by-
   firm engagement (skills programmes, designed subsidies).
2. **Local market size** — domestic middle class plus PTA-network market
   access. Constrains tools that need scale to amortise (tariffs, LCRs,
   procurement preferences).
3. **Fiscal space** — ability to raise tax revenue or borrow affordably.
   Constrains the resource-intensive tools (investment subsidies, wage
   subsidies, R&D grants).

This is a more honest framing than "Tier 1 vs Tier 3": some tools simply
aren't on the menu for low-bandwidth, small-market, fiscally tight
countries, regardless of theoretical promise.

## Empirical headline: who uses industrial policy how heavily?

- Upper-middle-income countries are the heaviest users of business
  subsidies, **averaging ~4.2% of GDP** (highest on record). They lean on
  tax expenditure rather than direct funding.
- Low-income economies target **13 industries** on average in their
  national development plans; high-income economies target **5**.
- Low- and middle-income countries also have higher import tariffs *and*
  more variance in tariffs across products — consistent with active
  product-level industrial policy.
- All 183 reviewed economies target at least one industry.
- World Bank conditional development-policy loans: since 2004, "most loans
  affirm rather than negate SOEs and subsidies" (a reversal from the
  earlier privatize-and-repeal-tariffs stance).

## The "Four I's" framework (slides 13–25)

The report organises tools into a hierarchy of preference. From most to
least preferred:

1. **Enabling Institutions** — accountable agencies insulated from
   political pressure; sound macro fundamentals (workforce education,
   infrastructure, fiscal stability).
2. **Public Inputs** — coordination assistance (industrial parks),
   skills development, market-access assistance, quality infrastructure.
   Targeted at specific market failures.
3. **Microeconomic Incentives** — subsidies, tariffs, procurement
   preferences. Treated as last-resort tools because they are fiscally
   costly and prone to retaliation.
4. **Macroeconomic Interventions** — exchange-rate devaluation, R&D tax
   credits. Caution flagged; more research needed.

This hierarchy is the actual ranking; the popular "industrial parks are
Tier 1" gloss in press coverage is a fair compression of "parks are the
flagship Public Input."

## The 12-lesson menu (slides 13–25, the analytical core)

Each lesson is a tool plus a case study plus a "principles of practice."
Reproduced compactly because this is the part that does the most work.

1. **Industrial parks solve coordination failures.**
   Evidence: Abagna, Hornok & Mulyukova 2025 *J. Dev. Econ.* on 10 African
   economies — parks increased shift away from agricultural jobs, durable-
   goods consumption, and educational attainment for nearby households.
   Principles: governments may pay upfront but recoup quickly; locate near
   labour pools and ports/transport hubs; bundle with utilities and
   schooling for park workers.

2. **Skills development, market-access assistance, and quality
   infrastructure solve information asymmetries.**
   Evidence: Costa Rica's industry-specific training to attract Intel
   (Spar 1998); Latin American export promotion (Volpe Martincus &
   Carballo 2008); Benin pineapple quality infrastructure (IFC 2023).
   Principles: bundle services; pair with parks and innovation subsidies;
   independent agencies for standards/metrology/accreditation.

3. **Production subsidies should be tied to output, wages, or investment
   depending on what the binding constraint is.**
   Evidence: Korean Heavy and Chemical Industry drive (Choi & Levchenko
   2025; Kim, Lee & Shin 2021; Lane 2025); Romania payroll-tax exemption
   for IT workers (Manelici & Pantea 2021); US wind production tax credit
   beat input subsidies (Aldy, Gerarden & Sweeney 2023).
   Principle: subsidise inputs only when you know which inputs constrain
   growth; otherwise subsidise output.

4. **Innovation subsidies are effective and efficient even when they
   produce mostly adoption, not invention.**
   Evidence: Brazil's Embrapa agricultural research — benefits 17× costs
   (Akerman, Moscona, Pellegrina & Sastry 2025). Brazil's Finep grants
   produced foreign-technology adaptation more than original invention
   (De Souza & Garber 2025).
   Principles: simple/fast applications; rigorous evaluation built in.

5. **Import tariffs can accelerate industry growth when there are
   resources and a large local market.**
   Evidence: Irwin 2000 on the US Tariff Act of 1890 tinplate tariffs
   (>70%) — domestic production dominant 10 years later, but with a
   consumer-surplus loss to canners and roofers; absent the tariff the
   industry would have emerged within ~20 years anyway as iron-bar prices
   fell.
   Principle: don't tariff raw materials or capital equipment.

6. **Public procurement can spark innovations with demonstration
   effects.**
   Evidence: Mensah, Wankuru & Kirui 2025 on Kenya — four years after
   first procurement, beneficiary firms had 27% higher productivity and
   10% more employees; the effect is comparable to the gains from
   trading with a multinational. Mazzucato & Rodrik 2023 on
   conditionalities.
   Principles: attach innovation conditionalities; collect bid data; ensure
   contestability.

7. **Technology-transfer quid-pro-quo works but requires a large market.**
   Evidence: China's 50% foreign-ownership cap in autos enabled domestic
   capability development without harming foreign investors' core engine
   business (Bai, Panle, Shengmao & Li 2025); Suzuki Maruti in India.
   Principle: only use this if the market is big enough to keep FDI on
   the table.

8. **Commodity export bans and local-content requirements shape up- and
   downstream markets — partially.**
   Evidence: Indonesia nickel-ore export ban raised domestic value added
   but no steel import substitution yet (Kee & Xie 2025). Bauxite ban
   has been slower to attract aluminium smelting.
   Principle: try subsidies/public inputs first; only ban exports when
   you have a large export market share (nickel yes, bauxite no).

9. **Consumer-demand subsidies are hard to target.**
   Evidence: Mexico energy-efficient-appliances subsidy was effective
   (more upgrades) but inefficient (most upgraders would have done so
   anyway) — Boomhower & Davis 2014.

10. **Competitive exchange-rate devaluation requires central-bank
    capacity.**
    Evidence: Rodrik 2008 cross-country regressions on undervaluation;
    larger effect for developing economies.
    Principle: requires reserve accumulation; manage capital account.

11. **Automatic termination rules may end policies too early.**
    The conventional 3–5-year sunset can fire before learning-by-doing
    has played out; the US tinplate case took 10 years. The principle:
    set 3-year evaluation milestones with up to 10-year persistence,
    extend only with declining cost-per-unit evidence.

12. **Pro-competitive industrial policy is open to trade.**
    Aghion et al. 2015: subsidies are more effective when dispersed
    across many firms within an industry. Government cartels can work
    *if* members face export discipline (Weinstein 1995 on Japan; Barwick
    et al. 2025a on China shipbuilding); but WTO now prohibits export
    contingency.

## Institutions: the "MITI archetype" and its failures (slides 29–30)

Quoted in the briefing:

- **MITI archetype** (Johnson 1982): "a small, inexpensive bureaucracy
  staffed by the best managerial talent available in the system …
  educated in law and economics, but it would be preferable if they were
  not professional lawyers or economists … the term that best describes
  what we are looking for here is not professionals, civil servants, or
  experts, but managers."
- **East Asian Miracle** (World Bank 1993): "Foremost among them is
  technocratic insulation … the ability of economic technocrats to
  formulate and implement policies in keeping with politically formulated
  national goals with a minimum of lobbying for special favors from
  politicians and interest groups."
- **Counter-example, Ghana IDC** (Lewis, via Killick 1978): "The IDC has
  suffered greatly from outside interference … members of Parliament and
  other influential persons expecting staff appointments to be made
  irrespective of merit, redundant staff to be kept on the pay-roll,
  disciplinary measures to be relaxed in favour of constituents …."
- **Counter-example, Argentina Tierra del Fuego electronics**: regime
  established 1972 to end 2007 (35-year sunset); quality and price still
  not internationally competitive; sunset extended to 2038 (66 years
  total). The textbook case of failed termination discipline.

## Why subsidies/tariffs score so poorly

- Capture: difficult to cut once a constituency forms.
- Distortion: protect uncompetitive incumbents, which then under-invest in
  productivity.
- Coordination failure: when many countries subsidize the same sector, the
  price effect dominates the productivity effect (cf. Smith's
  "unprofitability" point about late-stage Chinese subsidies in EVs and
  solar).

## Why industrial parks score so well

- Locational sorting: park boundaries make incentives easy to monitor, so
  rents don't leak system-wide.
- Bundling: roads + power + customs + tax holiday addresses several
  binding constraints simultaneously, which a single subsidy line item
  cannot do.
- Self-financing path: parks "may pay upfront but recoup quickly by
  selling units to occupants" (slide 14).
- Replicability: doesn't require sector-specific bets or sophisticated
  forecasting capacity, so even thin-bench bureaucracies can run them
  (this is the point that maps onto the "government bandwidth"
  constraint).

## Caveats and limits

- The evidence base is broader than China and includes mixed cases (e.g.,
  African SEZs that under-performed). Industrial parks *as a class*
  outperform on average; that does not mean every park works.
- The report does not directly advance a poverty-reduction *causal* claim
  for SEZs at the household level — for that, the underlying Abagna /
  Hornok / Mulyukova paper is the load-bearing citation, plus the
  Vietnamese SEZ literature (see `lay-tafese-tran-2025-vietnam.md`).
- The briefing flags that most causal evidence measures *effectiveness*
  (sales, employment) not *efficiency* (welfare > cost) — a real
  limitation for cost-benefit-style policy design.

## How the report relates to Krugman's targeting scepticism

Slide 26 quotes Krugman 1983 approvingly: *"While there is a valid case
for targeting grounded in economic theory, the theoretical basis is too
complex and ambiguous to be useful given the current state of
knowledge."* The report's response is not to abandon targeting but to
proxy positive spillovers with "newness": diversifying the economy and
moving up the value chain. This is the closest thing the report has to a
sectoral-selection rule.
