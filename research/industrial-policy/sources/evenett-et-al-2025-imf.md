## Bibliographic info

## Why it appears here

This is the second of the two recent multilateral reviews Smith cites
(the first being Fernandes & Reed at the World Bank). It supplies the
panel-data view of *what governments are actually doing* between 2009
and 2023, while the World Bank report supplies the *how-to* menu. The
two together are the basis for Smith's "export promotion good, import
substitution bad" framing.

## Methodology

- Extends the **New Industrial Policy Observatory (NIPO)** dataset from
  2009 to 2023.
- Uses large-language-model techniques to classify policy *motivations*
  (competitiveness, climate, supply-chain resilience, national security,
  geopolitical) at scale.
- Implemented by IMF in partnership with Global Trade Alert / NIPO.

## Key findings

1. **Widespread adoption.** Industrial policy is back in heavy use
   across both advanced and emerging economies.
2. **Tool composition.** Implementation is "primarily through subsidies
   and trade restrictions" — not through the public-input-style tools
   the Fernandes–Reed report would prefer.
3. **Structural break around 2020.** Two things changed at once: the
   *volume* of policy activity accelerated, and the *motivation* mix
   shifted toward supply-chain resilience, national security, and
   geopolitical tit-for-tat — on top of the older competitiveness and
   climate motivations.
4. **Targeting.** Post-2020 policies concentrate on dual-use and
   advanced-technology sectors and on their upstream inputs (critical
   minerals and raw materials). Support extends beyond sectors of
   traditional comparative advantage.
5. **Export promotion vs import substitution.** The relevant distinction
   for Smith's framing — that the former is much more promising than
   the latter — appears in the broader IMF analysis but is a normative
   inference from the data more than a single empirical claim. The
   paper documents that recent activity is heavily protectionist
   (subsidies and restrictions), not export-promotion-style.

## Implication for the analysis

The IMF paper is the empirical evidence that the global industrial-
policy turn since 2020 is *exactly* the kind of policy mix the
Fernandes–Reed report is most sceptical about: subsidies and trade
restrictions, motivated by geopolitical concerns, in advanced-tech
sectors. This reinforces the analysis-document point that late-stage
Chinese policy is not an outlier — it is the front of a broader rich-
country wave that has the same theoretical problems.

For the China-vs-SEA question, the IMF's documented policy turn is
relevant via two channels:
- It makes "China + 1" reshoring more attractive (geopolitical-risk
  tools).
- It makes the global subsidy environment more competitive, which
  tightens the unprofitability problem Smith warns about.
